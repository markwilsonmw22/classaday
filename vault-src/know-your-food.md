# Energy and calories

Food gives your body energy, measured in **calories** (strictly, kilocalories, written kcal or Cal). Every food label in Canada and the US shows calories per serving.

## Where calories come from

| Nutrient | Calories per gram |
|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 |
| Protein | 4 |
| Fat | 9 |
| Alcohol | 7 |
| Fibre | about 2 (partly digested) |

That's why a tablespoon of oil (about 14 g of fat) has around 120 calories, while a whole cup of broccoli has about 30.

## How much energy do you need?

It depends on your age, size, sex and how active you are. Many adults need somewhere between 1,800 and 3,000 calories a day. The **% Daily Value** on Canadian labels is based on a 2,000-calorie diet, as a reference point, not a target for everyone.

You don't need to count calories to eat well. Building balanced meals (chapter 4) and noticing hunger and fullness works for most people. Counting can help if you have a specific goal; if it makes you anxious about food, it isn't worth it.

## Calories aren't the whole story

A 200-calorie snack of nuts and a 200-calorie soft drink have the same energy but very different effects. The nuts bring protein, fibre, healthy fats and minerals that keep you full. The soft drink brings sugar and little else. **What a food contains** matters as much as how much energy it has.

# The big three: macronutrients

## Protein

Protein builds and repairs muscles, skin, hair, enzymes and hormones. It also keeps you full longer than carbs or fat.

- **Sources:** meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, tofu, tempeh, paneer, beans, lentils, chickpeas, nuts and seeds.
- **How much:** a common guide for healthy adults is about **0.8 g per kg of body weight per day** (about 56 g for a 70 kg adult). Older adults, active people and those recovering from illness often benefit from more.
- **Easy target:** include a palm-sized portion of protein at each meal.

## Carbohydrates

Carbs are your body's preferred quick fuel, especially for the brain and for exercise.

- **Whole carbohydrates** come with fibre, vitamins and minerals: whole grains, oats, brown rice, beans, lentils, fruit, vegetables.
- **Refined carbohydrates** have had much of that removed: white bread, pastries, sweets, sugary drinks.
- **Sugars** occur naturally in fruit and milk, and are added to many packaged foods. Health Canada and many health groups advise limiting added and "free" sugars.

### Fibre

Fibre is a carbohydrate you don't digest. It keeps your gut healthy, helps with regularity, slows the rise in blood sugar after meals and helps you feel full.

Health Canada's recommended intake is about **25 g a day for adult women and 38 g for adult men**. Most people eat far less.

**Easy ways to get more:** oats for breakfast, beans or lentils in soups and salads, whole-grain bread, fruit with the skin on, a handful of nuts, an extra vegetable at dinner.

## Fats

Fat carries flavour, helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and is essential for your brain and cells.

| Type | Found in | Choose |
|---|---|---|
| Unsaturated (mono and poly) | Olive oil, canola oil, avocado, nuts, seeds, fatty fish | **Most of the time** |
| Omega-3 (a polyunsaturated fat) | Salmon, sardines, trout, walnuts, chia, flax | **Often** |
| Saturated | Butter, cheese, fatty meat, coconut oil, palm oil | **In smaller amounts** |
| Trans | Industrially produced partially hydrogenated oils | **Avoid.** Canada banned them in foods in 2018 |

# Vitamins, minerals and water

Micronutrients are needed in small amounts, but you can't live without them. A varied diet with plenty of colour covers most needs.

| Nutrient | What it does | Good sources |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin A | Vision, immunity, skin | Carrots, sweet potato, spinach, eggs, milk |
| B vitamins (B6, B12, folate) | Energy, red blood cells, nerves | Whole grains, meat, eggs, legumes, leafy greens. **B12 only in animal foods or fortified foods** |
| Vitamin C | Immunity, healing, iron absorption | Peppers, citrus, strawberries, broccoli, kiwi |
| Vitamin D | Bones, immunity | Fatty fish, fortified milk and plant drinks, egg yolks. Hard to get from sun in a Canadian winter |
| Vitamin K | Blood clotting, bones | Leafy greens, broccoli, Brussels sprouts |
| Calcium | Bones, teeth, muscles | Milk, yogurt, cheese, fortified plant drinks, tofu made with calcium, canned salmon with bones |
| Iron | Carries oxygen in the blood | Red meat, chicken, beans, lentils, tofu, fortified cereals. Eat with vitamin C for better absorption |
| Potassium | Heart, muscles, blood pressure | Potatoes, bananas, beans, yogurt, leafy greens |
| Magnesium | Muscles, nerves, energy | Nuts, seeds, whole grains, leafy greens, dark chocolate |
| Zinc | Immunity, healing | Meat, shellfish, legumes, seeds, dairy |
| Iodine | Thyroid hormones | Iodized salt, dairy, seafood, eggs |
| Sodium | Fluid balance | Salt, and most packaged and restaurant food. **Most people eat too much** |

> **About supplements:** most healthy people can get what they need from food. Some groups may need supplements, for example vitamin D in winter, folic acid before and during pregnancy, and B12 for vegans. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement.

## Water

Your body is about 60% water. Needs vary, but a common guide is around **2–3 litres of fluids a day** for adults, including water from food. Drink more in hot weather and when exercising.

- Make **water your drink of choice**. Plain milk and unsweetened plant drinks are good too.
- Pale yellow urine is a simple sign you're drinking enough.
- Juice, soft drinks and many coffee-shop drinks contain a lot of sugar.

# Building a balanced plate

Canada's Food Guide suggests a simple picture for most meals:

{{svg:balanced_plate|The balanced plate, based on Canada's Food Guide.}}

- **Half the plate:** vegetables and fruits, in a range of colours.
- **A quarter:** whole-grain foods such as brown rice, whole-wheat pasta, oats, quinoa or whole-grain bread.
- **A quarter:** protein foods, choosing plant-based proteins more often.
- **To drink:** water.

This isn't a strict rule. A curry, a stir-fry or a sandwich can follow the same proportions without being on a plate.

## Glycemic index, in plain words

The **glycemic index (GI)** describes how quickly a carbohydrate food raises blood sugar.

- **Lower GI:** most beans and lentils, oats, barley, most fruit, whole-grain and sourdough breads, pasta cooked al dente.
- **Higher GI:** white bread, most breakfast cereals, white rice, potatoes, rice cakes, sugary drinks.

You can lower the effect of a meal by adding **fibre, protein and fat**: toast with eggs and avocado raises blood sugar more slowly than toast with jam.

# Reading food labels

## The Nutrition Facts table

{{svg:label_anatomy|How to read Canada's Nutrition Facts table. The US label shows the same information in a slightly different layout.}}

1. **Start with the serving size.** Every number below it is for that amount. If you eat twice as much, double everything.
2. **Look at calories** for that serving.
3. **Use the % Daily Value (%DV)** to judge quickly: **5% or less is a little, 15% or more is a lot.**
4. **Get less of:** saturated and trans fat, sugars, sodium.
5. **Get more of:** fibre, potassium, calcium, iron.
6. **Compare products** by making sure the serving sizes are similar.

## The ingredient list

Ingredients are listed **by weight, from most to least**. If sugar or salt is in the first three, it's a big part of the food. In Canada, sugars are grouped together in brackets after the word "Sugars", so you can see all the sources at once.

### Other names for sugar

Agave, brown rice syrup, cane juice, corn syrup, dextrose, fructose, glucose, high-fructose corn syrup, honey, invert sugar, maltose, maple syrup, molasses, sucrose, fruit juice concentrate.

## The front-of-package symbol (Canada)

Since **January 1, 2026**, most prepackaged foods in Canada that are high in **saturated fat, sugars or sodium** must show a black-and-white magnifying-glass symbol on the front saying "High in". It's a quick warning sign: if you see it, check the table.

## Allergens

Canadian labels must clearly declare the priority food allergens, gluten sources and added sulphites, either in the ingredient list or in a "Contains" statement:

Eggs · milk · mustard · peanuts · crustaceans and molluscs · fish · sesame seeds · soy · sulphites · tree nuts · wheat and triticale (and gluten from barley, oats, rye).

"May contain" statements are voluntary warnings about possible cross-contact.

## Claims, decoded

| Claim | What it means |
|---|---|
| "Source of fibre" | At least 2 g of fibre per serving |
| "High source of fibre" | At least 4 g per serving |
| "Low in sodium" | 140 mg or less per serving |
| "Reduced in…" | At least 25% less than the regular version, but it can still be high |
| "Natural" | Not a nutrition claim. Says nothing about health |
| "Multigrain" | Several grains, but not necessarily whole grains. Look for "whole grain" first in the ingredients |

# Food safety at home

Most foodborne illness at home comes from a few mistakes. Four habits prevent most of them: **clean, separate, cook, chill.**

## The danger zone

{{svg:danger_zone|Bacteria grow fastest between 4°C and 60°C (40°F–140°F).}}

- Don't leave perishable food at room temperature for more than **2 hours** (1 hour if it's very hot, above 32°C / 90°F).
- Keep your fridge at **4°C (40°F) or colder** and your freezer at **−18°C (0°F)**. Use a fridge thermometer.
- Thaw food in the fridge, in cold water or in the microwave, not on the counter.

## Safe internal cooking temperatures

Use a food thermometer in the thickest part. Colour isn't a reliable test. These are Health Canada's guidelines:

| Food | Cook to |
|---|---|
| Poultry pieces (chicken, turkey, duck) | 74°C (165°F) |
| Whole poultry | 82°C (180°F) |
| Ground meat: beef, pork, lamb, veal | 71°C (160°F) |
| Ground poultry | 74°C (165°F) |
| Beef, veal, lamb steaks and roasts | 63°C (145°F) medium-rare; 71°C (160°F) medium; 77°C (170°F) well done |
| Pork pieces and whole cuts | 71°C (160°F) |
| Fish | 70°C (158°F) |
| Egg dishes | 74°C (165°F) |
| Leftovers, stuffing, hot dogs | 74°C (165°F) |

## Clean and separate

- Wash hands with soap for 20 seconds before cooking and after handling raw meat, poultry, fish or eggs.
- Use separate cutting boards for raw meat and for ready-to-eat food.
- Don't rinse raw chicken; it splashes bacteria around the sink.
- Store raw meat on the **bottom shelf** of the fridge in a container, so it can't drip on other food.

## How long food keeps in the fridge

| Food | Fridge (4°C) |
|---|---|
| Raw ground meat and poultry | 1–2 days |
| Raw chicken pieces | 2–3 days |
| Raw steaks, chops and roasts | 3–5 days |
| Raw fish | 1–2 days |
| Cooked leftovers | 2–4 days |
| Cooked rice | 1–2 days; cool quickly and refrigerate within 1 hour |
| Opened deli meats | 3–4 days |
| Hard-boiled eggs | 7 days |

When in doubt, throw it out. Most frozen food stays safe indefinitely at −18°C; quality is best within a few months.

# Myths and facts

| Myth | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Carbs make you gain weight | Excess calories from any source do. Whole carbs with fibre are part of most healthy diets |
| Eating fat makes you fat | Fat is calorie-dense, but healthy fats are essential and help you feel full |
| Brown sugar and honey are much healthier than white sugar | They're all sugars. The tiny amounts of minerals don't make a real difference |
| You need to detox | Your liver and kidneys do that. Detox teas and juices aren't needed |
| Eating late at night causes weight gain | Total intake across the day matters more than the clock |
| Frozen vegetables are less nutritious | They're frozen soon after picking and are often just as nutritious as fresh |
| Eggs are bad for your heart | For most healthy people, eggs can be part of a balanced diet |
| Gluten-free is healthier | Only for people with coeliac disease or gluten sensitivity. Many gluten-free products are more refined |
| Skipping breakfast slows your metabolism | It doesn't. Eat breakfast if it helps you, skip it if it doesn't |

> **A note on special diets:** if you have diabetes, kidney disease, high blood pressure, food allergies or another medical condition, a registered dietitian can give advice tailored to you. In Canada, many provinces offer free dietitian advice by phone.
