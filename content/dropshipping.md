# M1 | Foundations | How dropshipping really works, what it costs, and how to choose a niche you can win in.

## D1 | How dropshipping works, and what it costs to start | 3 | How dropshipping works
@goal Understand the model, where the money goes, and set a starting budget you can afford to lose while you learn.
@lesson
Dropshipping is a way of running an online store where **you never hold stock**. A customer buys from your website, you pass the order to a supplier, and the supplier ships the product straight to the customer. You keep the difference between what the customer paid and what you paid the supplier, minus your costs.

{{fig:ds-flow|The two flows to keep in your head: money comes to you first, the parcel goes straight from supplier to customer.}}

That sounds simple, and the mechanics are. The hard part is everything around them: finding a product people want, getting it in front of them at a cost that still leaves profit, and keeping customers happy when you don't control the warehouse.

### What you're really building

You are building three things:

1. **A marketing machine.** Ads and content that put a product in front of the right people. This is where most of your time and money goes.
2. **A store that converts.** A website that turns visitors into buyers. A good store can double the result of the same ad spend.
3. **A reliable back office.** Suppliers, shipping, refunds and customer messages. This is what keeps your payment processor and your customers on your side.

### The honest pros and cons

| What's good | What's hard |
|---|---|
| Low start-up cost, no stock to buy | Margins are thinner than holding stock |
| Test many products quickly | You don't control shipping speed or quality |
| Work from anywhere | Ad costs can rise quickly |
| Skills transfer to any e-commerce brand | Competitors can copy a winning product fast |

Most stores that fail do so for one of three reasons: they spend on ads before knowing their numbers, they pick products with no real demand, or they make promises about delivery the supplier can't keep. This course is built to avoid all three.

### Your starting budget

Treat your first budget as **tuition**: money you expect to spend while you learn. A lean but realistic split:

| Item | Lean | Comfortable |
|---|---|---|
| Shopify plan (first months) | $40 | $80 |
| Domain name | $15 | $15 |
| Product samples (3 products) | $60 | $150 |
| Apps (reviews, email, upsell) | $0 | $60 |
| Ad testing | $350 | $1,100 |
| Buffer for refunds and surprises | $35 | $95 |
| **Total** | **$500** | **$1,500** |

Prices change, so check current plan pricing when you sign up. The ratio matters more than the exact numbers: **at least two thirds of your budget should go to testing ads**, because that is how you learn what sells.

### How the next 30 days run

- **Module 1 (Days 1–4):** the numbers, the paperwork and your niche.
- **Module 2 (Days 5–9):** product research until you have three products worth testing.
- **Module 3 (Days 10–12):** suppliers, samples and shipping policy.

## D2 | The numbers: margin, break-even and ad cost per sale | 3.5 | The numbers
@goal Calculate the profit on one order and the most you can pay in ads to get a sale without losing money.
@lesson
Every decision in this business comes back to one question: **how much can I spend to win a customer and still make money?** Today you learn to answer it in two minutes for any product.

### The parts of one sale

For each order you pay four kinds of cost:

- **Product cost (COGS):** what the supplier charges for the item.
- **Shipping:** what the supplier charges to deliver it.
- **Payment fees:** usually around 2.9% + 30¢ per order with card processors. Check your plan's exact rate.
- **Ad cost per sale (CPA):** total ad spend divided by number of sales.

{{fig:ds-unit|One $34.99 order, broken down. The red block is the one you control least and the one that decides if you make money.}}

### Three numbers to know by heart

**1. Gross margin before ads**

`Price − product − shipping − fees`

In the figure: $34.99 − $8.40 − $4.20 − $1.31 = **$21.08**.

**2. Break-even CPA**

This is the same number. If one sale costs you $21.08 in ads, you make exactly $0. Spend less per sale and you profit. Spend more and you lose.

**3. Break-even ROAS**

ROAS means *return on ad spend*: revenue divided by ad spend. Ad platforms report it, so you need to know your break-even:

`Break-even ROAS = Price ÷ Gross margin`

$34.99 ÷ $21.08 = **1.66**. If Meta shows a ROAS above 1.66, you're profitable on that product (before fixed costs like apps).

### Why price matters so much

Look at what happens if you sell the same product at $24.99 instead:

| | $24.99 price | $34.99 price |
|---|---|---|
| Product + shipping | $12.60 | $12.60 |
| Fees | $1.02 | $1.31 |
| Gross margin | $11.37 | $21.08 |
| Break-even ROAS | 2.20 | 1.66 |

The cheaper price almost halves the money you can spend to get a customer. Since ad costs are similar either way, **low-priced products are much harder to make profitable**. A good rule: your selling price should be **at least 2.5–3× your product-plus-shipping cost**, and ideally over $25.

### Average order value (AOV)

If some customers buy two, your average order goes up while ad cost per order stays about the same. That's why bundles and "buy 2, save 15%" offers (Day 18) are so powerful.

### Fixed costs

Apps, your Shopify plan and your domain are fixed monthly costs. Add them up and divide by expected monthly orders to see what each order must also cover. Early on, keep fixed costs under $100 a month.

## D3 | Setting up the business properly | 3 | Business setup
@goal Get the legal, banking, tax and bookkeeping basics in place so you can sell without nasty surprises later.
@lesson
Boring things done early save you from painful problems later: frozen payouts, tax bills you didn't expect, or a bank flagging your account. This lesson gives you the general picture. **Rules vary by country, province and state, so confirm the details with your local government website or an accountant.** This isn't legal or tax advice.

### 1. Business structure

- **Sole proprietorship:** the simplest start. You and the business are legally the same, and profits are taxed on your personal return. In Canada you can trade under your own name, or register a business name with your province (in Ontario, through ServiceOntario).
- **Corporation (Canada) or LLC (US):** separates your personal assets from the business and can help with tax later. It costs more to set up and maintain. Most people start as a sole proprietor and incorporate once the business is profitable.

### 2. Money accounts

- A **separate bank account and card** for every business expense and payout.
- **Shopify Payments** (available in Canada, the US and many other countries) for cards, Apple Pay and Shop Pay.
- **PayPal Business** as a second option. Many buyers prefer it.
- Expect new stores to have **payout holds or reserves** at first. Payment providers do this to protect against refunds. Keep enough cash to pay suppliers before payouts arrive.

### 3. Sales tax

- **Canada:** you must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales pass **$30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters** (the small supplier threshold). You may register earlier voluntarily. Provinces like BC, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Quebec have their own sales tax rules for online sellers.
- **United States:** sales tax depends on "nexus." Most states set economic nexus thresholds (commonly $100,000 in sales into that state per year). Shopify's tax settings help you track this.
- Shopify can **collect** tax at checkout once you tell it where you're registered. Collecting tax and **filing** it are separate: you still file returns.

### 4. Income tax and records

Keep every receipt: samples, apps, ads, your domain. Set aside **25–30% of profit** in a separate savings account for tax until an accountant tells you a better number for your situation.

### 5. Simple bookkeeping

You don't need accounting software on day one. A spreadsheet with these columns works: *Date, Description, Category, Money in, Money out, Receipt link.* Categories: Sales, Product cost, Shipping, Ads, Apps, Fees, Refunds, Other. When you're doing more than 20 orders a week, move to software like Wave, QuickBooks or Xero.

### 6. Things you must not sell

Avoid anything that could get your store or ad account shut down: counterfeit or branded items (no "Nike-style" logos), weapons, CBD and supplements with health claims, medical devices, items for children under three with small parts, and anything that makes medical, weight-loss or "cure" claims.

## D4 | Choosing a niche you can win in | 3.5 | Choosing a niche
@goal Score several niche ideas and choose one with real demand, healthy margins and room for your own angle.
@lesson
A **niche** is a group of people with a shared interest or problem: dog owners who groom at home, people who work from a desk all day, new parents, home bakers. Choosing a niche instead of selling random products helps you in three ways:

- Your ads get cheaper because you speak to one kind of person.
- Customers buy a second product from you, which raises profit per customer.
- You build a brand that's worth more than a single product.

### General store, niche store or one-product store?

| Type | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| General store | Testing many random products quickly | Looks untrustworthy, hard to build a brand |
| Niche store | Most beginners. Several related products for one audience | Needs a clear audience from day one |
| One-product store | A proven winner you want to brand properly | Feels empty if the product fails |

This course uses the **niche store**: you test three products for one audience, then turn the winner into a one-product page inside your niche store.

### What makes a good niche

1. **Passionate or in pain.** People spend on things they love (pets, hobbies) or problems that bother them (back pain, messy cables, sleep).
2. **Products sell for $25–$80.** Enough margin for ads.
3. **Visual products.** Things you can demonstrate in a 15-second video.
4. **Not dominated by one big brand.** If everyone just buys from the market leader, you'll struggle.
5. **Repeat purchases or accessories.** Refills, add-ons, a reason to come back.

{{fig:ds-niche|Plot your ideas on demand against margin. You want the top-right box: plenty of people searching and buying, with room to price well.}}

### How to score niches quickly

Use a 1–5 score for each:

- **Demand:** Search the niche on TikTok and Instagram. Are there videos with 100k+ views? Are brands running ads (check Meta Ad Library)?
- **Margin:** Can products sell for 2.5–3× their landed cost?
- **Passion/pain:** Do people comment, ask questions and share?
- **Content:** Can you easily film demos?
- **Repeat:** Is there a second product they'd want?

Add the scores. Anything below 17 out of 25 is a pass.

### Avoid these niches as a beginner

Electronics with batteries (returns, shipping restrictions), clothing (sizing returns), anything medical or supplement-based, and products owned by a big brand's trademark.

## D5 | What makes a winning product | 3.5 | Winning products
@goal Learn the checklist that filters out products that will lose money, so research time goes only to products with a chance.
@lesson
A "winning product" isn't magic. It's a product that passes a short list of tests, so that ads can sell it at a profit. Use this checklist on every idea from now on.

### The 10-point checklist

1. **Solves a problem or creates a "wow".** Either fixes something annoying (shedding hair everywhere) or makes people say "I need that" when they see it work.
2. **Easy to demonstrate in seconds.** If you need a paragraph to explain it, a scrolling viewer won't get it.
3. **Not easy to find in local stores.** If it's on the shelf at Walmart or Canadian Tire for $12, few people will pay you $35.
4. **Sells for $25–$80.** Below $25 the ad maths rarely works (Day 2). Above $80, buyers want reviews and faster shipping than most suppliers offer.
5. **2.5–3× markup or better** on landed cost.
6. **Small and light.** Cheaper to ship, fewer damages.
7. **Not fragile, not sized.** Glass breaks. Clothes and shoes come back.
8. **No trademark or brand issues.** No logos, character designs or patented look-alikes.
9. **Allowed on ad platforms.** No weapons, adult items, supplements, medical claims or "before/after" body content.
10. **Has an angle.** A specific person and moment you can show: "for golden retriever owners in shedding season."

A product needs to pass **at least 8 of 10**, and must pass 4, 5, 8 and 9 without exception.

### Demand signals that mean something

Likes alone mean little. Look for:

- **Comments asking "where can I buy this?"** or "link?"
- **Several different stores running ads** for the same product. One store could be luck; five means it sells.
- **Ads that have been running for 30+ days.** Nobody keeps paying for an ad that loses money. Meta Ad Library shows when each ad started.
- **Rising search interest** on Google Trends over the last 12 months.
- **Real order counts** on AliExpress or CJ, with recent reviews and photos.

### Saturation: how worried should you be?

A product that many stores sell can still work for you if you bring a **new angle**, a **better offer** or **better creative**. What kills you is selling the same product with the same video at the same price as twenty others. Early in your research, saturation is a small concern. During teardown (Day 8) you'll check whether you can stand out.

## D6 | Research method 1: social platforms and ad libraries | 4 | Social research
@goal Use TikTok, Instagram and the Meta Ad Library to find products that are already selling, and to understand why.
@lesson
The best evidence that a product sells is **someone else already paying to advertise it**. Today you'll learn to find that evidence on the platforms where you'll later advertise.

### Meta Ad Library (free, no account needed)

1. Go to the Meta Ad Library and choose your country, "All ads".
2. Search keywords from your niche: "dog brush", "deshedding", "grooming".
3. Click into results and look at the **"Started running on"** date. Ads running 30+ days, especially with **several versions** of the same ad, are making money.
4. Click the advertiser's page and look at their other ads. Successful stores often test variations of one winner.
5. Visit the store from the ad. Note price, offer and how the page is built.

Search phrases that sellers use in their copy, which find more stores: "free shipping", "50% off", "limited stock", "shop now", plus your niche word.

### TikTok

- **Search** your niche words and filter by "This month". Products with several viral videos from different accounts are trending.
- **TikTok Creative Center** shows top-performing ads by region and industry, plus trending hashtags. Use it to study hooks and formats.
- **TikTok Shop** listings show units sold for some products, a strong demand signal.
- Read comments. "Where do I get this?" is gold. "Mine broke after a week" is a warning.

### Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts

Search the same keywords. Reels are useful for seeing how the product looks in real homes and how real customers talk about it. Save the phrases they use; they'll go into your product page.

### Organise what you find

Every promising product gets a row in your Products tab with: link to the ad, link to the store, price, days the ad has run, number of ad variations, and your notes on the hook.

### What you're looking for

A product shows up **in at least two places** (for example, an ad running 45 days in the Ad Library plus organic videos on TikTok with buying comments). One source can be luck. Two is a pattern.

## D7 | Research method 2: marketplaces, suppliers and trends | 3.5 | Marketplace research
@goal Confirm demand from marketplaces and supplier data, and check that products have a stable supply.
@lesson
Yesterday you found products people are advertising. Today you confirm demand from a different angle: **what people are actually buying** on marketplaces, and whether suppliers can deliver.

### Amazon

- **Best Sellers** and **Movers & Shakers** lists by category. A product climbing Movers & Shakers is gaining demand.
- Read **3-star reviews**. They tell you exactly what disappoints buyers, which helps you pick a better supplier version and write a better page.
- Note the Amazon price. If Amazon sells the same item for $12 with next-day delivery, you need a strong angle or a different product.

### AliExpress and CJdropshipping

- Search the product. Look at **orders** count, rating (aim for 4.6+), number of reviews with photos, and store age.
- Compare 3–5 listings of the same product. Prices vary a lot; quality does too.
- Check **shipping options** to your main market: look for lines that deliver in 7–12 days with tracking, or suppliers with stock in a US or Canadian warehouse.

### Google Trends

Compare your product keyword over **5 years** and **12 months**:

- Steady or rising over 5 years: **evergreen**. Good for a brand.
- Spiky, with a sharp peak: **trend or season**. Can make quick money, but plan your exit.
- Falling: avoid unless you have a fresh angle.

Also check **seasonality**: paw cleaners spike in rainy and snowy months; pool products in summer. Launch a seasonal product 4–6 weeks before its peak.

### Product research tools

Paid tools (various "winning product" databases and ad spy tools) can speed this up, but they show everyone the same products. Use free methods first. If you buy a tool later, use it to confirm, not to choose for you.

### The supply check

A product with demand but unreliable supply is a trap. Before moving on, each product should have:

- At least **two suppliers** that sell it
- A shipping option with **tracking** to your main country
- Clear photos and specs (size, material, weight)

## D10 | Supplier options compared | 3.5 | Supplier options
@goal Understand the main ways to source dropshipped products and choose a starting supplier for each of your three products.
@lesson
Your supplier is the part of your business you don't control, so choose carefully. Here are the main options and how they compare.

### The main sourcing routes

| Option | How it works | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AliExpress via DSers** | DSers is a Shopify app that imports AliExpress products and sends orders in bulk | Testing cheaply, huge range | Variable quality, 8–20 day shipping, many sellers per product |
| **CJdropshipping** | Agent-style platform with its own warehouses (incl. US) | Testing and early scaling, branded packaging later | Prices change; confirm shipping lines |
| **Zendrop / AutoDS** | Apps with supplier catalogues, automation and some US stock | Automation and faster shipping options | Monthly fees; check per-product prices |
| **Spocket and similar** | Mostly US/EU suppliers | Faster shipping, higher quality | Higher product cost, lower margin |
| **Private agent** | A sourcing agent buys from factories and ships for you | Scaling a proven winner | Needs volume (usually 10–30+ orders a day) |

### What "landed cost" really includes

Always compare **product + shipping to your main market**. A $4.90 product with $6.50 shipping is worse than a $6.90 product with $3.80 shipping.

### Shipping lines and times

Suppliers offer different shipping methods. Look for:

- **Tracked** shipping only. Untracked parcels mean refund disputes you can't win.
- Realistic times: **7–12 business days** to North America from China on good lines; **3–7 days** from a US warehouse.
- Shipping that's allowed for your product (batteries, liquids and magnets often need special lines).

### Start simple

For testing, use **DSers with AliExpress** or **CJdropshipping**. Both connect to Shopify, cost nothing to start and let you switch suppliers later without changing your store. Once a product proves itself (around 10+ orders a day), ask an agent for a quote.

## D12 | Shipping, delivery promises and returns | 3 | Shipping & returns
@goal Write shipping and returns policies that are honest, protect your payment account and keep customers calm.
@lesson
Most dropshipping complaints are about **delivery time**. Customers don't hate waiting 10 days nearly as much as they hate being promised 3 and waiting 10. Set expectations clearly and you'll avoid most refund requests.

### Your delivery promise

Calculate: **processing time + shipping time + 2 days buffer.** If processing is 1–2 days and shipping 8–12, promise **10–15 business days** for the China line, or **4–8 business days** from a US warehouse.

Show that promise:

- On the product page, under the add-to-cart button
- In the shipping policy
- In the order confirmation email
- In the shipping confirmation email with the tracking link

### Shipping prices

Choose one approach:

- **Free shipping** with shipping cost built into the price (simplest, converts well)
- **Free shipping over a threshold** (e.g. $50) to encourage two-packs
- **Flat rate** (e.g. $4.95) if your margin is tight

### Returns and refunds policy

A clear policy reduces chargebacks. Include:

1. **Time window:** e.g. 30 days from delivery.
2. **Condition:** unused, in original packaging.
3. **Damaged or wrong items:** a photo by email, replaced or refunded at no cost.
4. **Change of mind:** customer pays return shipping, or you offer a partial refund to keep the item (often cheaper than shipping it back to China).
5. **How to start:** an email address and what to include (order number, photos).
6. **When refunds are issued:** e.g. within 5 business days of approval.

Check your local consumer protection rules; some places require specific rights for online purchases.

### Where do returns go?

Never send customers a supplier's address in China. For low-cost items, it's usually best to **refund or replace without asking for the item back**. For higher-priced items, use a local returns address (your own or a returns service) and resell or check the item.

### Lost parcels

If tracking hasn't updated in 10 days, contact the supplier and reship. Keep a "lost parcel" line in your budget; it happens.

@goal Create a simple, consistent brand: name, logo, colours, fonts and voice, so the store looks trustworthy from the first second.

## D16 | The product page that sells | 4.5 | Product page

@lesson

Benefit first, then product: *"Self-Cleaning Deshedding Brush, Removes up to 90% of Loose Undercoat"* only if you've tested that claim. Otherwise: *"Self-Cleaning Deshedding Brush for Double-Coated Dogs"*. Keep it under 70 characters.

### Images and video

1. **Problem:** one or two sentences about the frustration, in the customer's words (from your 3-star review research).

5. **Proof:** real reviews, your own tests, photos from your sample.

7. **FAQ:** shipping time, which dogs it suits, cleaning, returns.

- No fake reviews or review-import apps that copy someone else's reviews. Collect real ones from your first customers (Day 27).

- No medical or health claims.
- A **compare-at price** only if the product genuinely sold at that price or a fair market comparison supports it.

## D19 | Your marketing plan: customer, angles and channels | 3 | Marketing plan
@goal Define your customer in detail, write ten angles to test, and decide how you'll split paid and organic marketing.
@lesson
Ads don't sell products. **Angles** do. An angle is the specific reason a specific person wants your product right now. The same brush can be sold as "less hair on the sofa", "a calmer grooming routine" or "save $80 a month at the groomer". Each angle attracts different people and needs a different ad.

### Your customer avatar

Write a one-page profile of your main buyer:

- Who they are (age range, life situation, where they live)
- The problem in their words (from comments and reviews)
- What they've tried before and why it didn't work
- What they'd love to feel or have instead
- What makes them hesitate to buy (price, trust, delivery time)
- Where they spend time online

### Write ten angles

Use these angle types:

1. **Pain:** the problem in a vivid moment (*"black sweater, white dog"*)
2. **Money saved:** compare with the alternative (*"$80 a month at the groomer"*)
3. **Time saved**
4. **Satisfying demo:** the "wow" moment itself
5. **Gift:** for a person who has the problem
6. **Identity:** *"for husky people"*
7. **Comparison:** your product vs the old way
8. **Social proof:** customer reactions (once you have them)
9. **Seasonal:** shedding season, muddy winter walks
10. **Emotional:** bonding, care, calm

### Paid vs organic

- **Paid ads** (Meta, TikTok) give you speed and data. You pay for every test.
- **Organic content** (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) is free but slow and unpredictable. It's also the cheapest way to find which hooks work before you pay to promote them.

A good beginner split: **post organic content daily** and **run one paid test** with your best angles. Winning organic videos often become your best ads.

### Pick one main ad platform

Start with **Meta (Facebook and Instagram)** if your customer is 25+; **TikTok** if your customer is younger or your product is very visual and trending. Don't run both at once with a small budget.

## D20 | Filming and editing ads with your phone | 4 | Filming ads
@goal Film and edit your first three UGC-style videos, each built on one of your test angles.
@lesson
The ads that work best in this business don't look like ads. They look like a real person showing a real product on their phone. You can make them yourself today.

{{fig:ds-ad|Five beats in a 30-second ad. The hook decides whether anyone sees the rest.}}

### The five beats

1. **Hook (0–3s):** a moment, question or bold statement that stops the scroll. Show the most visual part of the problem or result immediately.
2. **Problem (3–8s):** show the pain clearly.
3. **Demo (8–18s):** the product solving it, close up.
4. **Proof (18–24s):** the result, a reaction, a comparison.
5. **Offer + CTA (24–30s):** what to do and why now (guarantee, bundle, season).

### Hook ideas

- *"POV: you own a husky and own black clothes"*
- *"I was paying $90 a month for this"*
- Start mid-action: the button press with fur sliding off
- *"Things I wish I'd bought sooner as a dog owner"*
- A surprising number: *"This came off ONE dog"*

Write at least **ten hooks** for each video. You can film one body and swap hooks to create several ads.

### Filming setup

- Vertical 9:16, 1080p or 4K, 30fps
- Natural light from a window in front of you
- Clean background; real home settings are good
- Tap to lock focus and exposure on the product
- Film each shot 3 times; film more close-ups than you think you need
- Record 2–3 seconds before and after each action, for easy cutting

If samples haven't arrived yet, script and storyboard today, and film the moment they arrive.

### Editing in CapCut

1. Cut every pause. Pace should feel fast.
2. Add **captions** (auto-captions, then correct mistakes). Most people watch without sound.
3. Add a text hook on screen in the first frame.
4. Use a trending or royalty-free sound at low volume under any voiceover.
5. Export at 1080×1920.

### Creators

When you have budget, pay creators on platforms that connect brands with UGC creators to make videos with your product. Send them a short brief with your angle, hook ideas and a shot list. Make sure you have rights to use their videos as ads.

## D23 | Launching your first test campaign | 4 | Launch your ads
@goal Build and launch a structured test campaign with a clear budget, so your spend produces answers, not just clicks.
@lesson
Your first campaign isn't about making money yet. It's about **learning which product, angle and hook can sell profitably**. A structured test gives you clean answers.

{{fig:ds-campaign|A simple test: one campaign, three ad sets, three ads each.}}

### Campaign settings

- **Objective:** Sales (Meta may show this as an Advantage+ sales campaign, or a manual sales campaign; manual gives you more control while testing).
- **Conversion event:** Purchase.
- **Budget:** set at the **ad set** level for testing, so each ad set gets a fair share.

### Ad set settings

- **Budget:** around **$20 a day per ad set**, or whatever lets you spend about **one break-even CPA per ad set per day**. For a $21 break-even CPA, $20–25/day is right.
- **Location:** the country (or two) you ship fastest to.
- **Audience:** broad. Age 18–65+ with no interests, or one interest that defines your niche. Meta's algorithm finds buyers better than manual targeting in most cases.
- **Placements:** Advantage+ placements, or Reels and Feeds if you want to keep it simple.

### Ads

- 2–3 ads per ad set, each a different hook or video.
- **Primary text:** 2–4 short lines, the angle in plain words, the offer, the guarantee.
- **Headline:** the benefit in under 40 characters.
- **Destination:** your product page (not the homepage).
- Add **UTM parameters** so you can see ad traffic in Shopify analytics.

### Budget for the test

Plan to spend **2–3× your break-even CPA per ad** before judging it. With 9 ads and a $21 CPA, a full test is roughly $400–$550 over 5–7 days.

### Rules during the test

- Don't touch anything for the first **48 hours** unless something is clearly broken.
- Check results once a day at the same time.
- Keep a **Test log** tab: date, ad set, spend, CPM, CTR, CPC, add to carts, purchases, CPA, ROAS.

### The metrics, in plain English

| Metric | Means | Rough beginner benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| CPM | Cost per 1,000 impressions | Varies by country; just track trend |
| CTR (link) | % who click | Above 1% is decent, 2%+ is strong |
| CPC | Cost per click | Lower is better; under $1 is good |
| Add-to-cart rate | % of visitors who add to cart | 5%+ suggests the page works |
| CPA | Ad cost per purchase | Below your break-even CPA |
| ROAS | Revenue ÷ spend | Above your break-even ROAS |

Benchmarks vary hugely by niche and country. Your break-even numbers are the only ones that decide profit.

## D24 | Reading results: kill, keep or scale | 3.5 | Reading results
@goal Learn to read ad data and make calm, rule-based decisions about which ads to stop, keep and scale.
@lesson
Most beginners lose money not because their ads are bad but because they make **emotional decisions**: killing an ad after one bad day, or doubling budget after one good day. Today you replace feelings with rules.

{{fig:ds-decision|A simple decision tree. Wait until an ad has spent at least one break-even CPA before you decide anything.}}

### Read the funnel from top to bottom

When an ad isn't selling, find **where** it's failing:

1. **Low CTR (under 1%)** → the hook or creative isn't stopping people. Fix the first 3 seconds.
2. **Good CTR, low add-to-cart rate** → the page or price doesn't match the ad's promise. Fix the product page or offer.
3. **Good add-to-cart, few purchases** → checkout problem: shipping cost surprise, trust issue or delivery time. Fix checkout.
4. **Purchases but CPA above break-even** → close; improve the offer (bundle), page or creative.

### Kill rules

Turn off an ad when it has spent:

- **1× break-even CPA** with no add-to-carts and CTR under 1%
- **2× break-even CPA** with add-to-carts but no purchase
- **3× break-even CPA** with CPA still above break-even

### Keep rules

Keep running ads that are near break-even and improving, especially early on. Data from the first few days is noisy.

### Scale rules

An ad set with **CPA below break-even for 3+ days** and at least 3–5 purchases can scale:

- **Vertical:** raise budget by about **20% every 48 hours**. Big jumps reset Meta's learning.
- **Horizontal:** duplicate the winning ad into a new ad set with a different audience or country, or add new creatives with the winning hook.

### Attribution reality check

Ad platforms and Shopify won't agree exactly. Use Shopify as your source for **revenue** and Meta for **which ads** drove it. Compare total spend to total Shopify revenue daily to make sure the business is profitable overall.

### When a product fails

If every ad set for a product fails after a full test, the product or angle doesn't work right now. That's a **successful test**: you learned something for a few hundred dollars. Move to product two.

### Try it yourself

Enter one ad set's numbers and the rules above make the call for you.

## D25 | Email flows that recover lost sales | 3.5 | Email flows
@goal Set up the four automated email flows that bring back visitors who didn't buy and turn buyers into repeat customers.
@lesson
Most visitors won't buy on their first visit. Email lets you follow up for free. A good set of automated emails often adds **10–20% more revenue** from the same traffic.

### Tool choice

- **Shopify Email:** free allowance each month, built in, enough to start.
- **Klaviyo:** the standard for e-commerce email, free up to a small list size, more powerful flows and reporting.

Either works. Start with what you can set up today.

### Grow your list

- A simple popup offering a reason to join: 10% off, or your free guide. Show it after 8–10 seconds or on exit intent, not instantly.
- The marketing checkbox at checkout (Day 17).
- Always get **clear consent**. In Canada, CASL requires consent and an easy unsubscribe; in the US, CAN-SPAM has similar requirements.

### The four flows

**1. Welcome flow** (when someone joins your list)

- Email 1, instantly: welcome, the discount or guide, your best seller.
- Email 2, day 2: your story and why you started.
- Email 3, day 4: social proof or how-to content, reminder the discount expires.

**2. Abandoned checkout** (started checkout, didn't finish)

- Email 1, after 1 hour: "Still deciding?" with the product image and a button back to checkout.
- Email 2, after 24 hours: answer the top objection (delivery time, guarantee).
- Email 3, after 48 hours: a small incentive if your margin allows, or the guarantee again.

- Email 2, on delivery or about 10 days later: how to use it, tips.
- Email 3, 14 days after delivery: ask for a review with photos.
- Email 4, 30 days: a related product.

### Writing good store emails

- Subject lines short and specific. Test two.
- One main message and one button per email.
- Sound like a person, using your brand voice.
- Mobile first: short paragraphs, big button.

## D26 | Order fulfilment and your daily routine | 3 | Fulfilment routine
@goal Set up a reliable way to fulfil orders, sync tracking and run a 30-minute daily routine.
@lesson
When orders start coming in, consistency becomes everything. A missed order or a missing tracking number turns a happy customer into a refund request.

### How fulfilment works with your supplier app

1. A customer orders on Shopify.
2. The order appears in DSers or CJ.
3. You check it and **pay the supplier** for it (manually or with auto-pay).
4. The supplier ships and adds a tracking number.
5. The app syncs tracking back to Shopify, which emails the customer automatically.

### Set it up properly

- **Map products** in the app so each Shopify variant links to the right supplier variant.
- Add **order notes** for the supplier: "No invoice or price in parcel. Ship in box, not bag."
- Choose the **shipping method** per country to match your promises.
- Turn on **automatic tracking sync**.
- Keep your supplier account funded (or a card on file) so orders don't sit unpaid.

### The 30-minute daily routine

Do this at the same time every day:

1. **Orders (10 min):** fulfil all new orders, check any that are unpaid or stuck.
2. **Tracking (5 min):** check orders older than 5 days without tracking; message the supplier.
3. **Customers (10 min):** answer every email and comment (Day 27).
4. **Numbers (5 min):** update your profit sheet (Day 29) and test log.

### Problem orders

- **No tracking after 5 days:** message the supplier.
- **Tracking stuck for 10+ days:** reship or refund, then claim from the supplier.
- **Wrong address:** contact the supplier immediately to change it; if shipped, contact the customer.

### When to automate more

At 20+ orders a day, consider auto-fulfilment, an agent who fulfils from stock, and a tracking page app with branded updates.

- Day 1 of ads: 3 orders. She fulfils them in CJ by 9pm. Tracking arrives two days later and syncs to Shopify.
- One customer typed "Mississauga" as the province. She emails the customer, fixes the address before the supplier ships, and adds a note to always check addresses.
- Her daily routine runs 8:30–9:00pm, after dinner, before checking ads.
@tasks

- Choose shipping methods per country and turn on tracking sync (20 min)

## D27 | Customer service, refunds, reviews and chargebacks | 3 | Customer service
@goal Handle customer messages quickly and kindly, decide refunds sensibly, collect real reviews and keep chargebacks low.
@lesson
In dropshipping, customer service is your strongest weapon. Big stores reply in days with templates. You can reply in hours, like a human.

### Response templates (customise each time)

Save these in your email tool:

- **Where is my order?** Tracking link, current status, honest expected date.
- **Delivery delay:** apology, update, and what you're doing about it.
- **Damaged or wrong item:** apology, ask for a photo, offer replacement or refund.
- **Change of mind:** your policy, the simplest option.
- **Product question before buying:** answer clearly, link to the product.

Always use the customer's name, answer the actual question first, and sign with your name.

### Making refund decisions

Ask: what costs less, and what keeps the customer happy?

- Item under ~$20 landed cost and damaged → refund or reship without asking for it back.
- Late delivery but tracking shows it's moving → reassure, offer a small partial refund if they're upset.
- Lost parcel → reship or refund, then claim from the supplier.

### Chargebacks

A chargeback is when a customer disputes the charge with their bank. Too many and your payment provider may hold or close your account. Keep them low:

- Statement descriptor that matches your store name (Day 13).
- Fast replies, so customers come to you instead of their bank.
- Clear delivery promises and tracking.
- When one happens, respond with evidence in Shopify: order details, tracking showing delivery, emails with the customer, your policies.

### Collecting real reviews

- Ask 14 days after delivery (your post-purchase flow).
- Make it easy: one click, star rating, optional photo.
- Offer a small thank-you for **photo** reviews (e.g. 10% off the next order) without requiring a positive review.
- Show reviews honestly, including the occasional 4-star.

Never post fake reviews or buy them. Many countries treat fake reviews as illegal deceptive marketing.
@demo
**A real exchange from Priya's week 2:**

> **Customer:** It's been 9 days and my brush still hasn't come.
>
> **Priya:** Hi Daniel, thanks for checking in. Your parcel cleared customs yesterday and is with Canada Post now (tracking: [link]). It should arrive within 3 business days. If it isn't with you by Friday, reply here and I'll send a replacement right away. Sorry for the wait, Priya

The customer replied "Thanks for the quick update!" and later left a 5-star review mentioning the service.
@tasks
- Write and save your five response templates (45 min)
- Write your refund decision rules on one page (25 min)
- Set up chargeback evidence: where to find tracking, emails and policies quickly (20 min)
- Configure your reviews app to send the review request 14 days after delivery (25 min)
- Answer every open customer message and comment using your templates (25 min)
@deliverable
Saved templates, refund rules, a chargeback plan and automatic review requests.
@tip
Reply to complaints faster than to anything else. An angry customer who gets a kind, fast answer often becomes your most loyal one.

## D28 | Scaling what works | 3.5 | Scaling
@goal Learn how to grow a profitable product safely, through budget, new creatives, new audiences and better supply.
@lesson
Scaling means spending more while keeping CPA below break-even. Do it too fast and costs jump. Do it carefully and profit grows week by week.

### Before scaling, check

- CPA below break-even for **3+ days**, ideally with 15+ purchases
- Supplier can handle more volume without delays
- You have cash to pay suppliers before payouts arrive
- Customer service is under control

### Vertical scaling (more budget)

- Raise winning ad set budgets by **~20% every 48 hours**.
- If CPA rises above break-even for 2 days, step back to the last good budget.
- Consider moving winners into a campaign-level budget (Meta's Advantage campaign budget) once you have several winning ad sets.

### Horizontal scaling (more reach)

- **New creatives:** your winning hook with new footage, new creators, new formats. Creative fatigue is the main reason winning ads die; plan 3–5 new ads per week.
- **New audiences:** broad, lookalikes of buyers, and interest groups.
- **New countries:** English-speaking markets you can ship to well (US, Canada, UK, Australia). Check shipping times and currency settings first.
- **New platforms:** TikTok ads once Meta is stable.

### Raise profit per customer

- Improve the page (test a new first image or headline).
- Add or improve bundles and upsells.
- Send a monthly email to past buyers with a related product.

### Upgrade your supply

At around **20–30 orders a day** for one product:

- Ask your supplier or an agent for a **better price** at volume.
- Move stock to a **US or Canadian warehouse** for faster delivery.
- Add **branded packaging** or an insert card.

These steps turn a dropshipping test into a real brand.

### Watch your cash flow

Fast growth can empty your bank account: you pay ads and suppliers daily, while payouts may arrive days later. Keep a cash buffer of at least **two weeks of ad spend plus product costs**.
@demo
**Priya's week 3 scaling plan:**

- Ad set A (broad) has run 4 days at $18 CPA with 22 purchases. She raises it from $22 to $26, then to $31 two days later.
- She briefs a UGC creator to make two new videos using the "black leggings" hook with a golden retriever.
- She duplicates the winning ad into a new ad set targeting the UK, after confirming 7–11 day delivery there.
- Cash check: payouts arrive 2–3 days after sales; she keeps $600 in the business account as a buffer.
@tasks
- Check your winning products against the four "before scaling" conditions (20 min)
- Write a scaling plan for the next 14 days: budgets, dates and stop rules (40 min)
- Brief or film 3 new creatives using your winning hook (60 min)

- Calculate your cash buffer and make sure you have it (20 min)

@tip
Most "my ads stopped working" moments are creative fatigue. If frequency (how often the same person sees your ad) climbs above 3–4 and CTR drops, you need new creatives, not new audiences.

## D29 | Money: profit tracking, cash flow and taxes | 3.5 | Profit tracking
@goal Build a daily profit dashboard so you always know whether the business is actually making money.
@lesson
Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity. Lots of stores show big revenue screenshots while losing money. Your profit sheet keeps you honest.

### The daily profit sheet

One row per day with these columns:

| Column | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Shopify (total sales after discounts) |
| Orders | Shopify |
| Product + shipping cost | Supplier app (sum of order costs) |
| Payment fees | Shopify/PayPal fees (or estimate %) |
| Ad spend | Ads Manager (all platforms) |
| Refunds | Shopify |
| Apps + fixed costs | Monthly total ÷ days in month |
| **Profit** | Revenue − all costs |
| **Profit margin** | Profit ÷ revenue |
| **Blended ROAS** | Revenue ÷ ad spend |
| **AOV** | Revenue ÷ orders |

Blended ROAS (all revenue divided by all ad spend) matters more than the ROAS shown per ad, because it includes email sales and returning customers.

### Weekly review (Sunday, 30 minutes)

- Total profit and margin for the week
- Best and worst products and ads
- Refund rate (aim for under 5%)
- One thing to improve next week

### Cash flow

Keep a simple forecast of money in (payouts) and money out (ads, suppliers, apps) for the next 14 days. If it goes negative, slow scaling until it doesn't.

### Taxes and paying yourself

- Move **25–30% of profit** to your tax savings account monthly (Day 3).
- Track sales by region for sales tax thresholds.
- Pay yourself a fixed amount once profit is consistent for three months, not before.
- Have an accountant review your books before your first tax return.

## D30 | Your 90-day plan and building a real brand | 3 | Your 90-day plan
@goal Review what you've built, decide your next move with each product, and write a 90-day plan to grow it into a brand.
@lesson
You have a live store, tested products, real data and a system for running it. Today you decide what comes next.

### Review your 30 days

Answer honestly:

1. Which product had the lowest CPA? Which angle and hook drove it?
2. What did customers say: in emails, comments and reviews?
3. Where did you lose the most money, and what did you learn?
4. Which tasks took most of your time? Which could be automated?

### Choose your path

**A. You have a profitable product.** Scale it (Day 28), improve supply, add accessories and build the brand.

**B. You have a product near break-even.** Spend the next 2–3 weeks on creative and offer: 10 new hooks, a stronger bundle, a better first image.

**C. Nothing has worked yet.** That's common. Test your next product from your shortlist with the same method. Your store, setup and skills are already built, so the next test is cheaper and faster.

### Building a real brand

The stores that last move from "a product" to "a brand customers come back to":

- **Branded packaging and inserts** once volume allows
- **Your own product photos and videos** everywhere
- **A product range** for the same customer
- **Content** that helps, not just sells: guides, tips, community
- **Email list** as your most valuable asset; it's the one channel you own
- **Faster shipping** through local warehouses
- Eventually, **private label**: your own version of the product, with your logo, made to your spec

### Your 90-day plan

Write three 30-day blocks, each with one main goal, three key actions and one number to hit. Keep it on one page.
