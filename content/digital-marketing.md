# M1 | Foundations & your agency | How digital marketing really works, the numbers behind it, and the agency you're going to build.

## D1 | How digital marketing actually works | 3.5 | How marketing works
@goal Understand the customer journey, the three kinds of media and the ten numbers every marketer uses, so the rest of the course has a map.
@lesson
Digital marketing sounds like a hundred different jobs: SEO, Reels, ads, email, funnels, "content". It's really one job: **help the right people find a business, trust it and buy from it, using the internet.** Every tool in this course is just a different way of doing that.

### The customer journey

Nobody goes from "never heard of you" to "here's my credit card" in one step. People move through stages, and each stage needs different marketing.

{{fig:dm-funnel|The funnel. Each stage has its own job, its own channels and its own numbers. Most struggling businesses only market at one stage.}}

| Stage | What the customer is thinking | Typical channels | Number to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | "Oh, that exists." | Short videos, ads, PR, influencers | Reach, impressions, views |
| **Interest** | "Tell me more." | Posts, blog articles, YouTube, Google search | Engagement, clicks, time on page |
| **Consideration** | "Is this the right one for me?" | Reviews, comparisons, case studies, retargeting ads | Profile visits, saves, leads |
| **Conversion** | "I'll buy / book / call." | Landing pages, Google Ads, offers, email | Conversion rate, cost per acquisition |
| **Loyalty** | "I'll come back and tell my friends." | Email, SMS, loyalty, community, great service | Repeat rate, reviews, referrals |

Think of it like a party. Awareness is getting invited. Interest is showing up. Consideration is chatting with the host. Conversion is staying for dinner. Loyalty is coming back next week and bringing friends. An agency that only sends invitations (posts) but never helps with dinner (website, offer, follow-up) will lose clients.

### Owned, earned and paid media

| Type | What it is | Examples | Superpower | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Owned** | Channels you control | Website, email list, blog, Google Business Profile | Free to use, yours forever | Slow to grow |
| **Earned** | Attention others give you | Reviews, shares, press, word of mouth, organic search rankings | Most trusted | Hard to control |
| **Paid** | Attention you buy | Meta ads, Google Ads, TikTok ads, sponsored posts | Fast and measurable | Stops when the money stops |

Great marketing uses all three together: paid ads bring people to an **owned** website, where good service creates **earned** reviews, which make the next ad work better.

### The ten numbers

You'll meet these in every report, every client call and every ad account. Learn them now and you'll sound like a pro by Friday.

| Metric | Means | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Impressions** | Times something was shown | counted by the platform |
| **Reach** | Unique people who saw it | counted by the platform |
| **CPM** | Cost per 1,000 impressions | spend ÷ impressions × 1,000 |
| **CTR** | Click-through rate | clicks ÷ impressions × 100 |
| **CPC** | Cost per click | spend ÷ clicks |
| **Conversion rate** | % of visitors who did the goal action | conversions ÷ visitors × 100 |
| **CPA / CPL** | Cost per acquisition (or per lead) | spend ÷ conversions |
| **ROAS** | Return on ad spend | revenue ÷ ad spend |
| **CAC** | Customer acquisition cost, all-in | all marketing costs ÷ new customers |
| **LTV** | Lifetime value of a customer | average order × orders per year × years × margin |

The golden rule of marketing maths: **a business can afford to grow when LTV is comfortably bigger than CAC.** A common target is LTV at least three times CAC.

### What an agency actually sells

Clients don't buy "posts" or "SEO". They buy outcomes: more calls, more bookings, more sales, less stress. Your job is to pick the right channels, do the work well and **prove the result with numbers**. That last part is what keeps clients paying you for years.

## D2 | Marketing fundamentals: customer, positioning and offer | 3.5 | Customer & offer
@goal Learn the three things every campaign depends on (who it's for, why them, and what they get) and write them for a real business.
@lesson
Here's a secret most agencies learn the hard way: **you can't out-post a bad offer.** Before any SEO or Reels, every client needs three things clear: the customer, the positioning and the offer. Get these right and every channel works better.

### 1. The ideal customer profile (ICP)

An ICP describes the people who are most valuable to the business and easiest to win. Not "everyone". Be specific:

- **Who:** age range, life stage, job, location
- **Problem:** what's annoying, painful or missing for them right now
- **Trigger:** what moment makes them search or buy (a burst pipe, a new baby, a first apartment)
- **Objections:** what makes them hesitate (price, trust, time)
- **Where they hang out:** platforms, groups, places, publications
- **Words they use:** phrases from reviews, comments and calls

The best source of these words is real customers. Read Google reviews (theirs and competitors'), Reddit threads, Facebook group posts and support emails. Copy the exact phrases.

### 2. Jobs to be done

People don't buy a product, they "hire" it for a job. Nobody wants a drill, they want a shelf on the wall. Ask: **what job is the customer hiring this business to do?**

- The café's job: "Give me a calm place to work for two hours that feels like a treat."
- The plumber's job: "Make the emergency go away today, without being ripped off."

Once you know the job, the marketing writes itself. The café should sell calm and Wi-Fi, not "artisanal beans". The plumber should sell speed and honesty, not "quality plumbing services".

### 3. Positioning

Positioning is the answer to "why you instead of the alternatives?" Fill in this sentence:

> For **[customer]** who **[need or trigger]**, **[business]** is the **[category]** that **[key benefit]**, unlike **[alternative]**, because **[proof]**.

### 4. The offer

An offer is everything the customer gets for what they pay, and the reasons to act now. A strong offer has:

| Part | Café example | Plumber example |
|---|---|---|
| Core product | Coffee, food, workspace | Repair or installation |
| Bonus | Free refill for laptop workers before 11am | Free camera inspection with any drain job |
| Guarantee | "If you don't love it, we'll remake it" | "Fixed price before we start. No surprises." |
| Urgency (honest) | Seasonal drink, limited time | Same-day slots, first come first served |
| Easy next step | Order ahead on the app | One-tap call button, online booking |

Never fake urgency or scarcity. Real limits (seasonal menus, booking slots) work just as well and keep trust.

### 5. Brand voice

Three words that describe how the business talks, plus three it never sounds like. Corner Grind: *warm, witty, local*; never *corporate, preachy, try-hard*. Write them down; you'll use them for every caption.
@demo

## D3 | The agency model: niche, services and pricing | 3.5 | Agency model
@goal Decide what kind of agency you'll run, who you'll serve, what you'll sell and how much you'll charge.
@lesson
"I do marketing for anyone who needs it" is the slowest way to grow an agency. Today you'll make three decisions that make everything else easier.

### Decision 1: your agency model

| Model | What it means | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Specialist** | One service, many industries (e.g. "Google Ads only") | Fast to get great at, easy to explain |
| **Niche** | Many services, one industry (e.g. "marketing for dentists") | Clients trust you faster, word of mouth spreads |
| **Full-service** | Many services, many industries | Bigger retainers, but hardest to start |
| **Productized** | Fixed package, fixed price (e.g. "12 Reels a month, $990") | Simple to sell, easy to deliver with a team |

The fastest start for most beginners: **pick a niche, lead with one service, add more later.**

### Decision 2: your niche

Score possible niches from 1 to 5:

- **Need:** do they rely on new customers from online? (plumbers, dentists, cafés, gyms, real estate: yes)
- **Money:** can they afford $800–$3,000 a month? A new customer must be worth a lot to them.
- **Reach:** can you find and contact 200+ of them within driving distance or online?
- **Interest:** will you enjoy making content about them for a year?
- **Proof:** do you already know someone in this industry who could become your case study?

Home services (plumbing, HVAC, roofing, renovation), health clinics (dental, physio, chiropractic), restaurants and cafés, fitness studios and real estate agents are common starting niches because one new customer is worth a lot.

### Decision 3: your services

| Service | What you deliver | Typical monthly price for small local businesses* |
|---|---|---|
| Social media management | Content plan, 8–20 posts and Reels, community replies, report | $800–$2,500 |
| Local SEO | Google Business Profile, citations, reviews, local pages, report | $600–$1,500 |
| Full SEO | Technical fixes, content, links, report | $1,000–$3,000+ |
| Paid ads management | Campaign setup, creative, optimisation, report | $500–$2,000 + ad spend, or 10–20% of spend |
| Email marketing | Flows, monthly campaigns, list growth | $500–$1,500 |
| Website (project) | New site or redesign | $1,500–$8,000 one time |
| Strategy/audit (project) | A full audit and 90-day plan | $300–$1,500 one time |

*Ranges vary a lot by city, scope and experience. Use them as a starting point, then adjust to your market.

### Pricing models

- **Hourly:** easy to start, but punishes you for getting faster. Avoid for ongoing work.
- **Project:** fixed price for a defined result (website, audit).
- **Retainer:** fixed monthly fee for an ongoing scope. The backbone of a stable agency.
- **Performance:** paid per lead or a share of revenue. Only once you control the whole funnel and trust the tracking.

### The agency maths

Work backwards from what you need:

`Monthly revenue goal ÷ average retainer = clients needed`

`Clients × hours per client per month = hours needed`

If the hours don't fit in your week, either raise prices, narrow the scope, or plan to hire. A healthy small agency aims for a **gross margin of 50% or more** after paying any freelancers.

## D4 | Set up your agency: brand, legal and toolkit | 3.5 | Agency setup
@goal Give your agency a name, a professional presence, the paperwork basics and a lean tool stack, so you're ready to take on clients.
@lesson
You don't need an office or a logo designer to start. You need to look credible, be legally set up and have the right tools. All of it can be done this week for very little money.

### Name and brand

A good agency name is easy to say, easy to spell, has an available `.com` or `.ca`, and doesn't box you in if you expand later. Test it by saying it on the phone: "Hi, it's Riya from Maple and Pixel."

Brand basics: a simple wordmark logo (Canva), two brand colours, one font, a professional email on your domain, and a consistent profile photo across LinkedIn, Instagram and your site.

### Legal and money basics (Canada)

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Check your province's rules or speak to an accountant.

- **Business structure:** most agencies start as a **sole proprietorship**. In Ontario, register your business name through the Ontario Business Registry if it isn't your own legal name. Incorporate later when profit and risk grow.
- **GST/HST:** you must register once your taxable sales pass **$30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters**. In Ontario, you'll then charge 13% HST to Canadian clients.
- **Bank account:** a separate business account and card from day one.
- **Insurance:** ask a broker about **professional liability (errors and omissions)** and general liability insurance. Some clients will ask for proof.
- **Contracts:** never start work without a signed agreement (Day 28 gives you a template).
- **Records:** keep every receipt and invoice. Set aside 25–30% of profit for tax until an accountant gives you a better number.

# M2 | SEO from zero to advanced | How search works, keywords, on-page, technical, content, local SEO, links and the full SEO audit.

## D5 | How search engines work, and keyword research | 4 | Keyword research
@goal Understand how Google finds and ranks pages, then build a keyword map for a real business using free tools.
@lesson
**SEO (search engine optimisation)** means making a website easy to find, understand and trust, so it shows up when people search. It's the slowest marketing channel to start and the most valuable once it works, because the traffic keeps coming without paying per click.

### How Google works in three steps

{{fig:dm-search|Crawl, index, rank. SEO problems happen at one of these three steps, and the fix depends on which one.}}

1. **Crawl:** Google's bots (Googlebot) follow links across the web and download pages.
2. **Index:** Google reads each page, works out what it's about and stores it in a giant library.
3. **Rank:** when someone searches, Google picks the most relevant, useful and trustworthy pages from the library and orders them.

Think of Google as a librarian with billions of books. Crawling is the librarian finding your book. Indexing is putting it on the right shelf. Ranking is recommending it when someone asks a question. A page that isn't crawled or indexed can't rank, however good it is.

### Search intent: why people search

Every search has a goal. Match the goal, or you won't rank.

| Intent | The searcher wants to | Example | Best page type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Informational | Learn something | "why is my water heater making noise" | Blog post, guide, video |
| Navigational | Go to a specific site | "northfield plumbing" | Homepage |
| Commercial | Compare options | "best tankless water heater canada" | Comparison, review, guide |
| Transactional | Buy or book now | "emergency plumber near me" | Service page, product page |
| Local | Find a nearby business | "plumber london ontario" | Google Business Profile + local page |

The quickest way to check intent: search the keyword and look at what already ranks. If Google shows guides, write a guide. If it shows service pages and a map, you need a service page and a Business Profile.

### Keyword metrics

- **Search volume:** roughly how many searches per month. Tools give estimates, not exact counts.
- **Keyword difficulty (KD):** how hard it is to reach page one, usually 0–100. It's a tool's guess based on the links of pages that rank.
- **CPC:** what advertisers pay per click. A high CPC means the keyword makes money, which is a great clue for commercial value.
- **Long-tail keywords:** longer, specific searches ("emergency plumber london ontario sunday"). Lower volume, but easier to rank and more likely to convert.

### The free keyword toolkit

| Tool | Use it for |
|---|---|
| Google autocomplete | Type a seed word and note every suggestion; add a, b, c... after it for more |
| "People also ask" and related searches | Questions real people ask |
| Google Keyword Planner | Volume ranges and CPC (free inside a Google Ads account, no need to run ads) |
| Google Search Console | Keywords a site already shows up for, with clicks and positions |
| Google Trends | Seasonality and rising topics |
| AnswerThePublic / AlsoAsked | Question research |
| Semrush, Ahrefs, Ubersuggest | Volumes, difficulty, competitor keywords (free trials or limited free use) |

### The keyword map

A keyword map assigns **one main keyword (plus close variations) to one page**. It stops two pages competing for the same search and shows which pages are missing. Columns:

*Keyword · Intent · Volume · Difficulty · Current ranking page · Target page · Page type · Priority*

Prioritise keywords that are **high intent + reachable difficulty + clear business value**. For a local service business, money keywords ("[service] [city]") come first, then helpful questions.

## D6 | On-page SEO: pages that rank and convert | 3.5 | On-page SEO
@goal Learn every element of on-page SEO and optimise a real page so it's clear to Google and convincing to people.
@lesson
On-page SEO is everything you control on the page itself. It's where most quick wins live, because many small business sites skip the basics.

### What people see in Google

{{fig:dm-serp|A typical results page. Your title and description are your "ad" in organic search, so they should earn the click.}}

### The on-page checklist

| Element | Best practice | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Title tag** | Main keyword near the start, benefit, brand. About 50–60 characters so it isn't cut off | *Emergency Plumber in London, ON · 24/7 · Northfield Plumbing* |
| **Meta description** | A short ad for the page, about 150–160 characters. Google may rewrite it, but write one anyway | *Burst pipe or no hot water? Fixed prices, on-time techs and 400+ five-star reviews. Call 24/7 or book online.* |
| **URL** | Short, readable, keyword included | `/emergency-plumber/` |
| **H1** | One per page, matches the search | *Emergency Plumber in London, Ontario* |
| **H2/H3 headings** | Break the page into scannable sections that answer real questions | *How fast can you get here? · What does it cost?* |
| **Opening paragraph** | Confirms the searcher is in the right place within two lines | |
| **Body content** | Answers every question the searcher has, in plain language, better than competitors | |
| **Images** | Compressed, descriptive file names, alt text | `plumber-fixing-burst-pipe-london.jpg` |
| **Internal links** | Link to and from related pages with descriptive anchor text | "See our **water heater replacement** service" |
| **Calls to action** | Clear, repeated, mobile-friendly (tap to call) | |
| **Trust signals** | Reviews, licences, guarantees, real photos, author or company details | |

### E-E-A-T, in plain words

Google's quality guidelines talk about **Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust**. For a plumber that means: real photos of real jobs, licence numbers, years in business, named technicians, honest prices and genuine reviews. For a blog post, it means written or reviewed by someone who actually knows the subject. You can't fake it; you can show it.

### Writing for people first

- Lead with the answer, then explain.
- Use the customer's words from your voice-of-customer sheet.
- Add specifics competitors don't: prices or price ranges, timelines, process, photos, FAQs.
- Make the page easy to act on: phone number at the top, booking button, clear next step.

### Where to edit these on common platforms

- **WordPress:** install **Yoast SEO** or **Rank Math** to edit titles, descriptions and see a checklist.
- **Shopify:** "Search engine listing" section at the bottom of each product, collection and page.
- **Wix and Squarespace:** SEO settings in each page's settings panel.
- **Hand-coded / templates:** edit the `<title>` and `<meta name="description">` in the `<head>`.

### Keyword stuffing is dead

Repeating a keyword 30 times doesn't help and reads badly. Use the main keyword in the title, H1, URL and naturally in the first paragraph. Then write naturally, using related words and answering related questions.

## D7 | Technical SEO | 4 | Technical SEO
@goal Find and fix the technical problems that stop pages being crawled, indexed or loading fast, using Search Console, Screaming Frog and PageSpeed Insights.
@lesson
Technical SEO is the plumbing of a website (fitting, given our client). Nobody admires it, but when it breaks, nothing else works.

### 1. Crawling and indexing

| Item | What it is | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **robots.txt** | A file at `/robots.txt` that tells bots what not to crawl | That it doesn't block important pages or the whole site (`Disallow: /`) |
| **XML sitemap** | A list of the pages you want indexed, usually `/sitemap.xml` | That it exists, lists only live pages, and is submitted in Search Console |
| **noindex tag** | Tells Google not to index a page | That it isn't left on by accident (a common mistake after a site launch) |
| **Canonical tag** | Tells Google which version of a duplicate page is the main one | That each page points to itself or the right original |
| **Status codes** | 200 OK, 301 moved permanently, 404 not found, 5xx server error | Broken links (404s) and redirect chains |
| **HTTPS** | Secure connection | Every page loads on `https://` and HTTP redirects to it |

A simple `robots.txt`:

```
User-agent: *
Disallow: /cart/
Disallow: /admin/
Sitemap: https://northfieldplumbing.ca/sitemap.xml
```

### 2. Google Search Console (free, essential)

Verify the site (a DNS record is best: it covers every version of the domain), then use:

- **Pages report:** which pages are indexed and why others aren't.
- **URL Inspection:** test a single page, see how Google sees it, request indexing after big changes.
- **Sitemaps:** submit the sitemap.
- **Performance:** clicks, impressions, average position and the queries people used.
- **Core Web Vitals and HTTPS reports.**

### 3. Crawl the site with Screaming Frog

**Screaming Frog SEO Spider** crawls a site like Googlebot and lists every URL with its status code, title, description, H1, word count, canonical and more. The free version crawls up to 500 URLs, which covers most small business sites. Export and sort:

- Missing or duplicate titles and descriptions
- Missing or multiple H1s
- 404s and redirect chains
- Pages with very little content
- Images over 200 KB

### 4. Page speed and Core Web Vitals

Google measures real-user experience with **Core Web Vitals**. Good scores:

| Metric | Measures | Good |
|---|---|---|
| **LCP** (Largest Contentful Paint) | How fast the main content appears | 2.5 seconds or less |
| **INP** (Interaction to Next Paint) | How quickly the page responds to taps and clicks | 200 ms or less |
| **CLS** (Cumulative Layout Shift) | How much things jump around while loading | 0.1 or less |

Test with **PageSpeed Insights**. The usual fixes: compress and resize images (WebP), lazy-load images below the first screen, remove unused apps and plugins, use good hosting, set image dimensions so nothing jumps.

### 5. Mobile

Google mainly uses the mobile version of a site to rank it. Check: readable text without zooming, buttons that are easy to tap, no content hidden on mobile, no pop-ups covering the page.

### 6. Structured data (schema)

Schema is code that tells Google exactly what's on a page: a business, a product, a review, an FAQ, an event. It can make results richer and helps search engines (and AI assistants) understand the business. Add it as JSON-LD in the page `<head>`:

```html
<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Plumber",
  "name": "Northfield Plumbing",
  "url": "https://northfieldplumbing.ca/",
  "telephone": "+1-519-555-0142",
  "image": "https://northfieldplumbing.ca/images/van.jpg",
  "priceRange": "$$",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "streetAddress": "120 Example Rd",
    "addressLocality": "London",
    "addressRegion": "ON",
    "postalCode": "N6A 1A1",
    "addressCountry": "CA"
  },
  "areaServed": ["London", "St. Thomas", "Strathroy"],
  "openingHours": "Mo-Su 00:00-23:59"
}
</script>
```

Test it with Google's **Rich Results Test** and the **Schema Markup Validator**. Only mark up what's actually visible and true on the page.

### 7. Prioritise fixes

Not every warning matters. Fix in this order: anything blocking indexing → broken important pages → speed problems on key pages → duplicate titles and content → nice-to-haves.
@demo
**Riya's technical check of Northfield (one afternoon).**

- **Search Console Pages report:** 14 pages "Excluded by noindex tag". The web developer had left noindex on the service pages after a redesign. **Fixed in five minutes; the biggest win of the whole project.**
- **Screaming Frog:** 9 broken internal links to old URLs → 301 redirects added. 22 pages with the same title "Northfield Plumbing" → unique titles written.
- **PageSpeed Insights (mobile):** LCP 5.8 s. The homepage hero was a 4.1 MB photo. Resized to 1600 px WebP (180 KB) → LCP 2.1 s.
- **Schema:** none → Plumber schema added to the homepage and contact page, validated.
- **Sitemap:** existed but listed 30 old URLs → regenerated and resubmitted.

Within three weeks, the 14 service pages were indexed and impressions in Search Console tripled.
@tasks
- Verify a website in Google Search Console (use a site you control, or your agency site) and submit its sitemap (30 min)
- Review the Pages report and write down every reason pages aren't indexed (25 min)
- Install Screaming Frog, crawl the site and export issues for titles, descriptions, H1s, status codes and image sizes (45 min)

- Put every issue in a **Technical** tab ranked by priority (15 min)
@deliverable

@tip
After any website redesign, check for leftover noindex tags and broken redirects first. It's the most common reason a business's traffic suddenly drops, and the fastest fix you'll ever bill for.

## D8 | Content SEO and AI search | 3.5 | Content & AI search
@goal Plan content that builds topical authority, write a content brief, and learn how to get a business cited in AI answers as well as ranked in Google.
@lesson
Content SEO is how a site grows beyond a handful of service pages. Done well, it brings in people at the Interest and Consideration stages, months before they're ready to buy.

### Topic clusters

Instead of random blog posts, build **clusters**: one in-depth **pillar page** on a broad topic, linked to several **cluster articles** that each answer a specific question, all linking back to the pillar and the money page.

{{fig:dm-cluster|A topic cluster. The links between pages tell Google "this site covers water heaters thoroughly".}}

### The content brief

Never ask a writer (or an AI) for "a blog post about water heaters". Give a brief:

| Brief field | Example |
|---|---|
| Target keyword | tankless vs tank water heater |
| Search intent | Commercial: comparing options before buying |
| Reader | Homeowner in Ontario, old tank failing, worried about cost |
| Goal of the page | Help them decide; book a free quote |
| Must answer | Upfront cost, running cost, lifespan, space, how long hot water lasts, rebates |
| Outline | H2s and H3s |
| Unique angle | Real installed prices from our last 30 jobs in London |
| Internal links | Water heater service page, emergency page |
| CTA | "Get a fixed-price quote in 24 hours" |
| Length | As long as needed to answer fully; competitors average ~1,800 words |

### Using AI for content, properly

AI assistants are excellent for research outlines, first drafts, FAQs, meta descriptions and rewriting. But published content needs **real experience**: photos, prices, opinions, stories, data only this business has. Use this workflow:

1. Human: write the brief and gather unique material (photos, prices, quotes from the owner).
2. AI: draft from the brief and material.
3. Human expert: check every fact, add experience, cut fluff, match the brand voice.
4. Publish, then update every 6–12 months.

Google's guidance focuses on whether content is helpful and original, not whether a tool was used. Mass-produced, unchecked AI pages are a real risk to a client's site.

### Search is changing: AI answers

Google now shows **AI Overviews** (and an **AI Mode**) for many searches, and people also search inside ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Gemini. Many informational searches now end without a click to any website. That changes the job:

- **Be the source AI cites.** Answer questions clearly in the first sentence under each heading. Use lists, tables and specific numbers.
- **Show unique information:** original data, prices, case studies and expert quotes are what AI tools reference.
- **Be consistent everywhere.** The business name, services, address and phone should match across the website, Google Business Profile, directories and social profiles, so AI systems connect them as one entity.
- **Earn mentions:** reviews, local news, industry sites and Reddit discussions all shape what AI tools say about a business.
- **Don't abandon money pages.** People still click to call, book and buy. Transactional and local searches remain the most valuable for small businesses.

Track AI visibility simply: ask the major assistants your client's key questions every month ("best emergency plumber in London Ontario") and note whether they're mentioned, alongside Search Console data.

### Updating beats publishing

Refreshing an existing article that ranks on page two often brings faster results than writing a new one. In Search Console, filter for pages with high impressions and an average position between 8 and 20. Those are your refresh list.
@demo
**Riya's water heater cluster for Northfield.**

- **Pillar:** *Water Heaters in London, Ontario: Costs, Types and When to Replace* (links to the service page).
- **Clusters:** *Tankless vs tank* · *How long does a water heater last?* · *Water heater making noise: 6 causes* · *Rent or buy a water heater in Ontario?* · *What size water heater do I need?*

For the tankless article, the owner gives her real numbers from 30 installs. She drafts with an AI assistant from the brief and the data, then the owner checks every number and adds a story about a family of five who ran out of hot water every morning.

Two months later, the article appears as a cited source in Google's AI Overview for "tankless vs tank water heater ontario", and three readers book quotes from it in one month.

- Choose one topic for a cluster and list the pillar plus five cluster articles (25 min)
- In Search Console (or your keyword tool), find three pages with high impressions and position 8–20 for a refresh list (20 min)

@deliverable
A topic cluster plan, a refresh list, a full content brief and one edited article draft.
@tip
Every article should contain at least one thing a competitor can't copy: a real price, photo, number or story from the business. That's what both readers and AI search reward.

## D9 | Local SEO and Google Business Profile | 3.5 | Local SEO
@goal Get a local business into Google's map pack with a fully optimised Business Profile, a review system and consistent citations.
@lesson
For local businesses, the **map pack** (the three businesses with a map at the top of local searches) often gets more calls than the website. And it's where a small agency can deliver results fastest.

### How Google ranks local results

Google says local ranking is based on three things:

1. **Relevance:** how well the profile matches the search (categories, services, description, website content).
2. **Distance:** how close the business is to the searcher or the searched location.
3. **Prominence:** how well known and trusted it is (reviews, links, mentions, overall SEO).

You can't change distance, so you win on relevance and prominence.

### Optimising a Google Business Profile (GBP)

| Field | Best practice |
|---|---|
| Business name | The real name only. Adding keywords ("Northfield Plumbing – Best Plumber London") breaks Google's rules and can get the profile suspended |
| Primary category | The single most important choice. Pick the most specific match ("Plumber") |
| Secondary categories | Add every accurate extra one ("Water heater installation service", "Drainage service") |
| Services | List every service with a short description |
| Service area / address | Show the address if customers visit; otherwise set service areas and hide the address |
| Hours | Accurate, including holiday hours |
| Phone and website | A local number; link to the most relevant page (with UTM tags, Day 24) |
| Description | What you do, where, why choose you. No links or promotions |
| Photos and video | Real photos of team, vans, work, before and after. Add new ones monthly |
| Posts | Updates, offers and tips weekly or every two weeks |
| Products | Use for service packages with price ranges |

### Reviews: the engine of local SEO

Reviews affect ranking and, even more, whether people call. Build a system:

- **Ask every customer**, at the moment they're happiest (job finished, problem solved). A text or email with the direct review link works best.
- **Reply to every review**, positive and negative, within a few days. Thank people by name, mention the service, and handle complaints calmly and offline.
- **Never** buy reviews, write fake ones, offer discounts or gifts for reviews, or ask only happy customers (so-called review gating). These break Google's policies, can get reviews removed or the profile penalised, and fake reviews can break consumer protection law.

Review request template:

> Hi Sarah, thanks for having us out today! If Jake did a good job on your water heater, would you mind leaving a quick review? It helps local families find us: [link]. – The Northfield team

### Citations and NAP

A **citation** is any listing of the business's **N**ame, **A**ddress and **P**hone number (NAP) online. Make them consistent everywhere, letter for letter. Start with:

- Google Business Profile, **Apple Business Connect** (Apple Maps), **Bing Places**
- Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, Yelp, YellowPages.ca
- Industry directories (HomeStars for trades in Canada, RateMDs for clinics, TripAdvisor for restaurants)
- Local chamber of commerce and BIA (business improvement area) listings

Tools like **BrightLocal** and **Whitespark** can audit and build citations for you.

### Local landing pages

If a business serves several towns, create a genuinely useful page for each main area: local photos, jobs done there, local reviews, area-specific details. Avoid copy-paste pages with only the town name swapped; Google treats those as low quality.

Starting point: Northfield appeared in the map pack for "plumber london ontario" only within a few blocks of their shop, with 412 reviews but replies to none.

Riya's changes:
- Primary category stayed "Plumber"; added four accurate secondary categories.
- Added 14 services with descriptions; uploaded 40 real job photos.

An optimised Business Profile, a review request system, reply templates and a citation fix list.

## D10 | Link building and digital PR | 3.5 | Link building
@goal Understand which links help a site, earn real links for a local business, and run a simple outreach campaign.
@lesson
Links from other websites are like votes of confidence. When a respected site links to yours, Google takes it as a sign your page is trustworthy and useful. Links are still one of the strongest ranking signals, especially in competitive searches.

### Good links vs bad links

| Good links | Bad links |
|---|---|
| Relevant to the business or local area | From unrelated or spammy sites |
| From real sites with real readers | From link farms and private blog networks |
| Earned because the content or business is genuinely worth linking to | Bought, or swapped in bulk |
| Placed naturally in useful content | Hidden, in footers of hundreds of sites, or in comment spam |

Buying links or using link schemes breaks Google's spam policies and can get a site penalised. One link from the local newspaper is worth more than a thousand from junk sites.

### Link building tactics that work for small businesses

1. **Local relationships:** suppliers, partners, sponsors, associations, the chamber of commerce. Many have "members" or "partners" pages.
2. **Sponsorships and community:** youth sports teams, charity events, festivals. Do them because they're good for the business; the link is a bonus.
3. **Local press:** a genuinely interesting story (a free furnace for a family in need, a 50-year anniversary, hiring 10 apprentices).
4. **Expert sources:** reporters ask for expert quotes on journalist-request platforms such as Qwoted and Featured. Answer quickly with useful, specific quotes.
5. **Data and digital PR:** publish something only you have, like "The average cost of water heater replacement in London, Ontario (2026)", and pitch it to local media and blogs.
6. **Resource and directory pages:** "Home maintenance resources" pages run by cities, real estate agents or community groups.
7. **Broken link building:** find broken links on relevant pages, then suggest your page as a replacement.
8. **Unlinked mentions:** search for the business name; where it's mentioned without a link, ask politely for one.

### Outreach that gets replies

Short, personal and useful to them:

> **Subject:** Quick resource for your home buyers page
>
> Hi Tom, I noticed your "First-time home buyer checklist" links to a water heater guide that now shows a 404. We just published a guide on checking a water heater's age and condition before buying a home in London, with photos from real inspections: [link]. It might be a useful replacement. Either way, great checklist!
>
> Riya, Maple & Pixel (on behalf of Northfield Plumbing)

### Tracking links

Keep an **Outreach** sheet: site, contact, date, template, follow-up date, status, link URL. Check new links in Search Console (Links report) or Ahrefs Webmaster Tools (free for sites you own).

# M3 | Web scraping & data | Collect public web data safely with no-code tools and Python, and turn it into research and clean lead lists.

## D12 | Web scraping basics, the rules and no-code tools | 3.5 | Scraping basics
@goal Understand what web scraping is, when it's allowed, and how to pull data from websites into a spreadsheet without writing code.
@lesson
**Web scraping** means using software to collect information from web pages automatically, instead of copying and pasting by hand. For an agency it's a superpower: hours of research done in minutes.

### What agencies use scraping for

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| SEO audits | Pull every page's title, description and H1 from a client's site |
| Competitor research | Track competitors' prices, services, menus or new pages |
| Content research | Collect questions from forums or headlines from industry blogs |
| Lead research | Build a list of businesses in a niche from public directories |
| Review monitoring | Gather a client's reviews from different sites into one sheet |
| Reporting | Pull numbers from public pages on a schedule |

### How scraping works

{{fig:dm-scrape|Every scraper, no-code or Python, follows the same five steps.}}

1. **Request** a page (like your browser does).
2. **Receive** the HTML: the code that makes up the page.
3. **Select** the parts you want, using a **selector** (a pattern that points at elements).
4. **Clean** the data (trim spaces, fix prices, remove duplicates).
5. **Save** it to a sheet, CSV or database.

### The rules: scrape responsibly

Scraping public information is a normal business activity, but there are lines you must not cross. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

- **Read the terms of service.** Many sites forbid automated collection. Google, LinkedIn, Facebook and Instagram all prohibit scraping their services in their terms. Breaking terms can get accounts banned and create legal risk.
- **Check `robots.txt`.** It tells bots which areas they shouldn't crawl. Respect it.
- **Prefer official APIs.** If a site offers an API (a proper data feed), use it instead of scraping.
- **Go slow.** Add delays between requests (one per second or slower). Never hit a site hard enough to slow it down.
- **Identify yourself.** Use a clear user-agent with a contact email.
- **Personal information is different.** Names, personal emails and phone numbers of individuals are protected by privacy laws such as Canada's PIPEDA. Collect only what you need, for a legitimate business purpose, and never build lists of private individuals for marketing.
- **Copyright.** Facts (a price, an address) are generally fine to record; copying whole articles, photos or reviews to republish is not.
- **Never log in to scrape** private areas, and never get around paywalls, CAPTCHAs or blocks.

### Selectors in five minutes

Right-click any part of a page and choose **Inspect** to see its HTML. Then point at it with:

| Selector type | Example | Means |
|---|---|---|
| CSS: tag | `h1` | Every H1 |
| CSS: class | `.price` | Elements with class "price" |
| CSS: nested | `article.product h3 a` | Links inside H3s inside product articles |
| XPath: element | `//h1` | Every H1 |
| XPath: attribute | `//meta[@name='description']/@content` | The meta description text |
| XPath: class | `//p[@class='price_color']` | Paragraphs with that exact class |

### Scraper 1: every book on a practice site

**books.toscrape.com** is a website built for people learning to scrape, so you can practise freely. Save this as `books.py` and run `python books.py`:

```python
"""Collect every book on books.toscrape.com (a practice site) into books.csv."""
import csv
import time
from urllib.parse import urljoin

import requests
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup

START = "https://books.toscrape.com/"
HEADERS = {"User-Agent": "MapleAndPixel-learning-bot/1.0 (you@example.com)"}
RATINGS = {"One": 1, "Two": 2, "Three": 3, "Four": 4, "Five": 5}


def scrape_page(url):
    response = requests.get(url, headers=HEADERS, timeout=15)
    response.raise_for_status()
    soup = BeautifulSoup(response.content, "html.parser")

    books = []
    for card in soup.select("article.product_pod"):
        link = card.select_one("h3 a")
        rating_word = card.select_one("p.star-rating")["class"][1]
        books.append({
            "title": link["title"],
            "price": card.select_one("p.price_color").get_text(strip=True),
            "rating": RATINGS.get(rating_word, 0),
            "in_stock": "In stock" in card.select_one("p.availability").get_text(),
            "url": urljoin(url, link["href"]),
        })

    next_link = soup.select_one("li.next a")
    next_url = urljoin(url, next_link["href"]) if next_link else None
    return books, next_url


def main():
    url, all_books = START, []
    while url:
        books, url = scrape_page(url)
        all_books.extend(books)
        print(f"{len(all_books)} books so far")
        time.sleep(1)  # be polite: at most one request per second

    with open("books.csv", "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        writer = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=all_books[0].keys())
        writer.writeheader()
        writer.writerows(all_books)
    print(f"Saved {len(all_books)} books to books.csv")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

What each part does:

- `requests.get(...)` downloads the page. `raise_for_status()` stops with an error if the page fails.
- `soup.select("article.product_pod")` finds every book card. `select_one` finds the first match inside a card.
- `urljoin` turns relative links like `catalogue/page-2.html` into full URLs.
- The `while url:` loop follows the **Next** button until there isn't one. That's **pagination**.
- `time.sleep(1)` waits a second between pages.
- `csv.DictWriter` saves everything in a file Google Sheets or Excel can open.

### Scraper 2: a mini SEO crawler for any site

This one reads a site's sitemap, checks `robots.txt`, and records the status, title, description, H1 and word count of each page. Use it on your own or a client's site. Save as `seo_check.py` and run `python seo_check.py https://yoursite.com/`:

```python
"""Mini SEO checker: read a sitemap and record on-page basics for each URL."""
import csv
import sys
import time
import urllib.robotparser
import warnings
from urllib.parse import urljoin

import requests
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup, XMLParsedAsHTMLWarning

warnings.filterwarnings("ignore", category=XMLParsedAsHTMLWarning)  # sitemaps are XML; that's fine

SITE = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "https://example.com/"
HEADERS = {"User-Agent": "MapleAndPixel-SEO-check/1.0 (you@example.com)"}
LIMIT = 200  # pages to check at most

robots = urllib.robotparser.RobotFileParser(urljoin(SITE, "/robots.txt"))
robots.read()


def sitemap_urls(sitemap_url):
    page = requests.get(sitemap_url, headers=HEADERS, timeout=15)
    soup = BeautifulSoup(page.content, "html.parser")
    locs = [loc.get_text(strip=True) for loc in soup.find_all("loc")]
    if soup.find("sitemapindex"):  # a sitemap of sitemaps: open each one
        return [url for child in locs for url in sitemap_urls(child)]
    return locs


def check(url):
    if not robots.can_fetch(HEADERS["User-Agent"], url):
        return {"url": url, "status": "blocked by robots.txt"}
    response = requests.get(url, headers=HEADERS, timeout=15)
    soup = BeautifulSoup(response.content, "html.parser")
    title = soup.title.get_text(strip=True) if soup.title else ""
    desc_tag = soup.find("meta", attrs={"name": "description"})
    description = desc_tag.get("content", "").strip() if desc_tag else ""
    h1s = soup.find_all("h1")
    return {
        "url": url,
        "status": response.status_code,
        "final_url": response.url,
        "title": title,
        "title_length": len(title),
        "description": description,
        "description_length": len(description),
        "h1_count": len(h1s),
        "h1": h1s[0].get_text(strip=True) if h1s else "",
        "words": len(soup.get_text(" ", strip=True).split()),
    }


def main():
    urls = sitemap_urls(urljoin(SITE, "/sitemap.xml"))[:LIMIT]
    print(f"Checking {len(urls)} pages...")
    rows = []
    for url in urls:
        rows.append(check(url))
        time.sleep(1)
    fields = list(max(rows, key=len).keys())
    with open("seo_check.csv", "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        writer = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=fields)
        writer.writeheader()
        writer.writerows(rows)
    print("Saved seo_check.csv")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Open the CSV and sort by `title_length`, `h1_count` and `status` to spot problems in seconds.

### The rules: Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL)

CASL applies to any **commercial electronic message** (email, text, DM) sent to or from Canada. It's strict, and penalties can reach $1 million per violation for individuals and $10 million for businesses. This is general information, not legal advice; read the CRTC's guidance.

You need **consent** before sending. For cold outreach to businesses, the key route is **implied consent through conspicuous publication**. It applies only when **all** of these are true:

1. The person's email address is **published conspicuously** (for example, on the business's website or a directory),
2. It's **not accompanied by a statement** that they don't want unsolicited commercial messages, and
3. Your message is **relevant to their business role** (marketing services pitched to the owner or marketing manager: yes; a random product: no).

Every message must also:

- **Identify you:** your name, business name, and a mailing address plus a phone number, email or web address.
- **Include an unsubscribe** that works, and remove anyone who unsubscribes within **10 business days**.

Keep records: a screenshot or note showing where and when you found each address. The burden of proving consent is on the sender.

In the US, **CAN-SPAM** is less strict (opt-out based) but also requires honest subject lines, a physical address and a working unsubscribe.

## D15 | The social media platform playbook | 3.5 | Platform playbook
@goal Know what every major platform is good for, how its algorithm decides who sees a post, and which platforms each client actually needs.
@lesson
"We need to be on every platform" is how small businesses burn out. Each platform has a different audience, format and purpose. Your job is to choose the two or three that matter for each client and do them properly.

### How algorithms decide what people see

Modern feeds are mostly **interest-based**: platforms show posts to people likely to enjoy them, even if they don't follow the account. They test a post with a small group, measure the reaction, and show it to more people if it does well.

The signals that matter most across platforms:

- **Watch time and completion:** did people watch to the end, or re-watch?
- **Shares and sends:** did people send it to a friend? (One of the strongest signals.)
- **Saves:** did they want to come back to it?
- **Meaningful comments:** real conversation, not "nice pic".
- **Early engagement:** how the first test group reacted.

The lesson: make content worth watching to the end, worth sending to a friend, or worth saving. Follower counts matter less than they used to.

**Carousel structure (saves and shares magnet):**

- Film **vertical**, 1080p or 4K, 30 fps; clean the lens.
- Face a window for soft light; avoid overhead lights that cast shadows.
- Sound matters more than picture: use a cheap clip-on wireless microphone.
- Shoot **B-roll** (close-ups of hands, steam, tools, before/after) for every video.

### The short video structure

| Time | Job | Example (plumber) |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 s | **Hook**: visual + on-screen text that stops the scroll | Water spraying from a pipe: "Do this BEFORE you call a plumber" |
| 2–20 s | **Value**: show, don't just tell | Finding the main shut-off valve, turning it |
| 20–30 s | **Payoff + CTA** | Water stops. "Then call us. Same-day, fixed price." |

3. Put a text hook on screen in the first frame.
4. Use the platform's **commercial music library** for business accounts; trending songs from the personal library aren't licensed for brand use.
5. Export 1080 × 1920.

The best video in the world fails if the first line is boring. Copywriting is the skill that makes every other marketing skill work better.

### 15 hook formulas

| Formula | Example |
|---|---|
| The mistake | "You're storing your coffee beans wrong." |
| The number | "3 signs your furnace won't make it through winter." |
| The question | "Why does your shower go cold after 5 minutes?" |
| The bold claim | "This $12 part saves you a $400 plumber visit." |
| The POV | "POV: you just opened a café and the espresso machine breaks on day one." |
| The before/after | "This basement flooded on Monday. Here's Friday." |
| The secret | "What plumbers check first (and don't tell you)." |
| The comparison | "Tankless vs tank: which is cheaper over 10 years?" |
| The challenge | "I tried every latte on Richmond Row. Here's the ranking." |
| The story | "At 2am on Christmas Eve, Mrs. Patel's pipe burst..." |
| The warning | "Don't pour this down your drain this weekend." |
| The insider | "Baristas hate this order. Here's why." |
| The result | "We doubled our weekday regulars with one sign." |
| The relatable | "When the landlord says 'just jiggle the handle'..." |
| The direct | "Need a plumber in London today? Watch this first." |

### Caption frameworks

**PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solution**

> Cold shower again? 😩
> If your hot water runs out faster every month, sediment is probably building up in your tank. Left alone, it can crack the tank and flood your basement.
> A 30-minute flush fixes most of these. We do them every day, fixed price, same week. Book in bio.

**AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action**

> New on the menu: the maple pecan latte. 🍁
> Real Ontario maple syrup, toasted pecans, our house espresso.
> It tastes like the first cold morning of fall, in a cup.
> Here until the leaves are gone. Come try it this week.

**BAB: Before, After, Bridge**

> Before: a basement nobody wanted to go into.
> After: a dry, bright family room (swipe →).
> The bridge: a new sump pump, a waterproof membrane and 6 days of work. Thinking about your basement? Message us for a free assessment.

### Calls to action

Every post needs one clear next step. Match it to the goal:

- **Engagement:** "Save this for later." · "Send this to someone who needs it." · "Which one would you pick? Comment A or B."
- **Traffic:** "Link in bio." · "Tap the link in Stories."
- **Action:** "Show this post at the till." · "Call or book online today." · "DM us 'QUOTE'."

### Platform tone

- **Instagram:** short, warm, scannable. First line matters most (it's all people see).
- **TikTok:** casual, spoken, on-screen text does the heavy lifting.
- **Facebook:** friendly and local; questions and community.
- **LinkedIn:** a strong first line, short paragraphs, a real lesson or story, no hashtag piles.

### Hashtags and keywords

Use 3–5 **relevant** hashtags (Instagram itself recommends a small number) and put real keywords in the caption and on-screen text: "best latte in London Ontario" helps social search far more than #coffeelover.

### Accessibility

Add **alt text** to images, **captions** to every video, use emojis sparingly (screen readers read each one aloud), and keep text on images readable (high contrast, large size).

1. You draft the month in the calendar or scheduler.
2. The client reviews by a set date (for example, the 25th for next month), commenting directly on each post.
3. You make changes in one round.

| Task | Standard |
|---|---|

4. **Fix and explain** what happened and what's changing.
5. **Follow up** and resume normal posting only when it's calm.

## D20 | Influencers, UGC and partnerships | 3 | Influencers & UGC
@goal Find, vet and brief creators, run a compliant influencer or UGC campaign, and use partnership ads to turn creator content into ads.
@lesson
People trust people more than brands. Influencer marketing and **UGC (user-generated content)** borrow that trust. For local businesses, the best creators are often small, local and affordable.

### Creator tiers

| Tier | Followers | Typical strengths |
|---|---|---|
| Nano | 1K–10K | Very local, high trust, often happy with free product or small fees |
| Micro | 10K–100K | Strong niche audiences, good engagement, affordable |
| Mid / macro | 100K–1M | Reach and polish; costs rise fast |
| Mega | 1M+ | Big awareness; rarely worth it for local businesses |

**UGC creators** are different: they make content for the brand to post or run as ads, without posting it to their own audience. Great for ads that look natural.

### Finding creators

- Search local hashtags and locations ("#ldnont", "London Ontario food") on Instagram and TikTok.
- Look at who's already tagging the business or competitors.
- **TikTok One** (TikTok's creator platform) and Instagram's creator marketplace for brands with ad accounts.
- UGC platforms and freelance sites for UGC-only creators.

### Vetting

- **Engagement rate:** (likes + comments + shares + saves) ÷ followers × 100. For small creators, 3–6% or more is healthy; look for real comments, not just emojis.
- **Audience location:** ask for a screenshot of their audience insights. A London café needs London followers.
- **Fit and values:** read their last 30 posts. Would the brand be happy next to all of them?
- **Fake followers:** sudden follower spikes, comments in unrelated languages, or very low views compared with followers.

### The brief

One page: brand background, campaign goal, key message (one, not five), must-include (offer code, location, disclosure), must-avoid, deliverables and deadlines, approval process, usage rights, payment.

### The agreement

Write down: deliverables (e.g. 1 Reel + 3 Stories), posting dates, payment and timing, **usage rights** (can the brand reuse it as an ad, for how long, on which platforms), exclusivity (no competitor content for X weeks), approval rounds, and disclosure.

### Disclosure is the law

In Canada, the Competition Act prohibits misleading advertising, and the Competition Bureau and **Ad Standards' Influencer Marketing Disclosure Guidelines** say any paid or gifted relationship must be clearly disclosed. In the US, the **FTC** has similar rules. In practice:

- Use the platform's **paid partnership** label.
- Also put **#ad** or "Paid partnership with @brand" at the start of the caption (not buried in hashtags), and say it in videos.
- Free products count as a material connection and must be disclosed too.

### Turning creator content into ads

- **Meta partnership ads:** run ads from the creator's handle (with their permission), which often perform better than brand-handle ads.
- **TikTok Spark Ads:** boost a creator's organic post as an ad using an authorisation code.

Make sure the contract covers ad usage before you do this.

## D22 | Meta and TikTok ads for clients | 4 | Meta & TikTok ads
@goal Set up client ad accounts the agency way, build lead and sales campaigns on Meta and TikTok, and know how to judge and improve them.
@lesson
Paid social is the fastest way to put a client in front of new local customers. As an agency, you also need to set it up so the **client owns everything** and you can manage it safely.

### Account ownership: the agency rules

- The **client owns** the business portfolio, Page, Instagram, ad account and pixel, and **pays Meta directly** with their own card.
- Your agency gets **partner access** to their assets from your own business portfolio.
- Never run a client's ads from your personal ad account or card, and never ask for their Facebook password.

If you part ways, the client keeps their data and history. That honesty is a selling point.

### Tracking first

Before any campaign:

1. Install the **Meta pixel** on the website (or via the platform integration, e.g. Shopify's Facebook & Instagram app, or Google Tag Manager).
2. Turn on the **Conversions API** where possible (it sends events from the server, which is more reliable than the browser alone).
3. Set up the events that matter: Lead, Contact, Schedule, Purchase.
4. Test them in **Events Manager**.

### Campaign objectives

| Objective | Use it when | Local example |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | You want reach in an area at low cost | New café opening, seasonal launch |
| Traffic | You want visits to a page (use sparingly) | A blog guide, a menu |
| Engagement | You want messages, video views or interactions | "DM us for a quote" |
| **Leads** | You want form fills, calls or bookings | Furnace tune-ups, free consultations |
| **Sales** | You want purchases or tracked conversions on the website | Online orders, e-commerce |

For most local service clients, start with **Leads** (instant forms or website conversions). For e-commerce, **Sales**.

### Targeting in 2026: let creative do the work

Meta's AI (Advantage+ audiences and placements) is usually better than detailed manual interests. For local businesses:

- Set the **location** carefully: a radius around the business or the service area, people **living in** the area.
- Keep audiences **broad** within that area; let the creative attract the right people.
- Use **retargeting** for website visitors, video viewers and Instagram engagers.

### Special ad categories

Ads about **housing, employment, financial products and services, and social issues, elections or politics** must be declared as special ad categories, which limits targeting (age, gender, postcode-level targeting). A real estate agent's listings ads or a client's job ads fall here. Declaring them is mandatory.

### Lead ads that produce real customers

Meta's **instant forms** let people request a quote without leaving the app. To avoid junk leads:

- Use **higher intent** form type (adds a review step before submit).
- Ask 1–2 qualifying questions ("What do you need help with?", "When?").
- **Speed to lead:** send leads to the client's phone and CRM within minutes (Meta's CRM integrations or Zapier). Leads called within five minutes are far more likely to book than leads called the next day.

### Creative testing

Creative is the biggest lever. Test **3–5 ads per ad set**: different hooks, formats (video vs carousel vs image) and angles (price, speed, trust, offer). Keep winners, replace losers every 2–4 weeks as they fatigue.

### Budgets and the learning phase

Meta's delivery system "learns" for each ad set. It works best when an ad set gets around **50 optimisation events a week**. With small budgets, use fewer ad sets so each gets enough data, and avoid big edits that restart learning.

### TikTok ads

- **TikTok Ads Manager** with the same objectives logic (reach, traffic, leads, conversions).
- Install the **TikTok pixel** and Events API.
- **Spark Ads** promote organic posts (the brand's or a creator's, with an authorisation code), which feel native and usually outperform polished ads.
- Creative must look like TikTok: vertical, fast, people talking, captions.

### Reading results

Use the funnel from Day 1 and the decision rules from the Dropshipping course: low CTR means the hook isn't working; clicks but no leads means the page or form is the problem; leads but no sales means lead quality or follow-up is. **Cost per lead is not the final number; cost per customer is.** Ask the client how many leads became jobs.

## D23 | Google Ads | 4 | Google Ads
@goal Build a Google Search campaign that captures people actively looking for a service, and understand Performance Max, Demand Gen and Local Services Ads.
@lesson
Social ads interrupt people. **Google Search ads reach people already searching for what the client sells.** For emergency and high-intent services, it's often the most profitable channel.

### Agency setup

Create a free **Google Ads Manager Account (MCC)** for your agency and link each client's account to it. The client owns their account and billing; you manage it from one login.

### Account structure

`Account → Campaigns (budget, location, bidding) → Ad groups (a theme) → Keywords + Ads`

Keep ad groups tightly themed: one for "emergency plumber", one for "water heater replacement", one for "drain cleaning". Each gets ads written for that theme and sends people to the matching landing page.

### Keyword match types

| Match type | How you write it | Shows for | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broad | `water heater repair` | Searches related to the meaning, including variations | With smart bidding and good negatives |
| Phrase | `"water heater repair"` | Searches that include the meaning of the phrase | A good default for small budgets |
| Exact | `[water heater repair]` | Searches with the same meaning | Your proven best keywords |

### Negative keywords: your money saver

Negatives stop ads showing for irrelevant searches. Common ones for local services: *free, diy, how to, jobs, salary, course, training, cheap* (if premium), *manual, parts, youtube*, and competitor brand names (unless deliberate). Check the **Search terms report** weekly and add new negatives.

### Responsive Search Ads

You give Google up to **15 headlines (30 characters each)** and **4 descriptions (90 characters each)**; it tests combinations. Include the keyword, the benefit, proof and a call to action:

- *Emergency Plumber London ON* · *Fixed Price Before We Start* · *412 Five-Star Reviews* · *Same-Day Service, 7 Days* · *Call Now, Real People 24/7*
- *Burst pipe or no hot water? Licensed techs at your door fast. Fixed price, no surprises.*

### Assets (formerly extensions)

Add every relevant one: **sitelinks** (other services), **callouts** ("Licensed & insured", "Fixed pricing"), **call** asset (phone number), **location** (linked Google Business Profile), **structured snippets** (services list), images. They make ads bigger and usually improve CTR.

### Settings that waste money if you get them wrong

- **Location options:** target "**Presence**: people in or regularly in your targeted locations", not people merely "interested in" the area.
- **Networks:** untick the Display Network for Search campaigns.
- **Ad schedule:** match the client's hours (or 24/7 for emergency services that answer the phone).
- **Conversion tracking:** calls from ads, calls from the website (via Google forwarding numbers or tracking `tel:` clicks), and form submissions. Without it, smart bidding is guessing.

### Bidding

Start with **Maximize clicks** (with a max CPC limit) or **Maximize conversions** if tracking is solid. Once you have roughly 15–30 conversions a month, try **Target CPA**.

### Quality Score

Google rates keywords 1–10 based on **expected CTR**, **ad relevance** and **landing page experience**. Higher scores mean lower costs. Improve it with tight ad groups, the keyword in the ad, and a fast, relevant landing page.

### Other campaign types

| Type | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance Max** | One campaign across Search, YouTube, Display, Gmail, Maps, using Google's AI | E-commerce with product feeds; local businesses with strong conversion tracking |
| **Demand Gen** | Visual ads on YouTube (incl. Shorts), Discover and Gmail | Awareness and consideration with good creative |
| **Local Services Ads** | Pay-per-lead ads at the very top for trades and home services, with a Google verification badge | Plumbers, electricians, HVAC, cleaners and more, where available in the client's city and category |

### Budget maths

Estimate before you launch, and tell the client honestly:

`Monthly budget ÷ average CPC = clicks`
`Clicks × conversion rate = leads`
`Leads × close rate × average job value = revenue`

## D24 | Analytics: GA4, Tag Manager, UTMs and dashboards | 4 | Analytics & tracking
@goal Track every channel properly with GA4, Google Tag Manager and UTM links, and build a dashboard that shows clients what's working.
@lesson
Without tracking, marketing is a debate about opinions. With it, it's a conversation about results. Today you'll set up the measurement stack every agency client needs.

{{fig:dm-tracking|How a click becomes a number in the client's report. Every link you control gets UTM tags, and every valuable action becomes a key event.}}

### Google Analytics 4 (GA4)

GA4 measures what people do on a website. Setup:

1. Create a **property** for the client (in the client's own Google account; add your agency as an admin).
2. Add a **web data stream**; copy the **Measurement ID** (`G-XXXXXXX`).
3. Install it through Google Tag Manager (best) or the website platform's integration.
4. Keep **enhanced measurement** on (page views, scrolls, outbound clicks, site search, video, file downloads).
5. Mark the actions that matter as **key events** (GA4's name for conversions since 2024): generate_lead, phone click, booking, purchase.
6. Link GA4 to **Search Console** and **Google Ads**.

Reports you'll use most:

- **Reports → Acquisition → Traffic acquisition:** sessions and key events by channel and by session source/medium.
- **Reports → Engagement → Pages and screens:** which pages get visits.
- **Explore → Funnel exploration:** where people drop off.

### Google Tag Manager (GTM)

GTM lets you add and manage tracking codes without editing the website each time.

- **Tags:** what to send (GA4 event, Meta pixel event, Google Ads conversion).
- **Triggers:** when to send it (a page view, a click, a form submission).
- **Variables:** extra details (the URL clicked, the form ID).

Two tags every local business needs:

| Action | Trigger | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| **Phone click** | Click (just links) where Click URL starts with `tel:` | GA4 event `phone_click` (mark as key event) |
| **Form submitted** | Page view of the thank-you page, or a form submission trigger | GA4 event `generate_lead` (key event) + Google Ads conversion |

Always test in **Preview mode** before publishing, then check GA4's **DebugView** or Realtime report.

### UTM links

UTM tags tell GA4 exactly where a visit came from. Add them to every link you control: social bios, posts, emails, Google Business Profile website link, QR codes and influencer links.

`https://cornergrind.ca/menu/?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=fall_menu_2026&utm_content=maple_latte_reel`

| Parameter | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `utm_source` | The platform | instagram, facebook, google, newsletter |
| `utm_medium` | The type of traffic | social, paid_social, email, cpc, organic |
| `utm_campaign` | The campaign name | fall_menu_2026 |
| `utm_content` | Which link or creative | maple_latte_reel |
| `utm_term` | Keyword (mainly for paid search) | maple latte |

Rules: **lowercase**, no spaces, the same naming every time, and keep a **UTM sheet** for each client. Use Google's free Campaign URL Builder.

### Looker Studio dashboards

**Looker Studio** (free) connects to GA4, Search Console, Google Ads, Google Sheets and more. A good client dashboard has one page:

- Scorecards: sessions, key events (leads/calls), conversion rate, cost per lead (if ads)
- A trend chart: leads by week
- A table: leads by channel
- Top landing pages
- Search Console clicks and top queries

For Meta and TikTok data, either export to Google Sheets monthly or use a paid connector.

### Privacy and consent

Tracking must respect privacy law. In Canada, PIPEDA requires meaningful consent and transparency, and **Quebec's Law 25** requires consent for tracking technologies that identify, locate or profile people (with cookie banners commonly used). The EU's GDPR is stricter still. In practice: a clear **privacy policy**, a **cookie consent banner** where required (Google's **Consent Mode** adjusts tags based on choices), and no personal data sent to analytics. This is general information; clients should get legal advice for their situation.

## D25 | Email marketing and automation | 3.5 | Email marketing
@goal Build a permission-based email list, set up automated flows, write campaigns that get clicks, and make sure emails reach the inbox.
@lesson
Email is the channel a business **owns**. No algorithm decides who sees it, and for many businesses it produces the highest return of any channel. It's also an easy upsell for an agency.

### Choosing a platform

| Platform | Best for |
|---|---|
| **Mailchimp** | General small businesses, easy start |
| **Klaviyo** | E-commerce (deep Shopify integration, strong flows and segmentation) |
| **MailerLite** | Simple, affordable, good for creators and small businesses |
| **Brevo** | Email + SMS, generous sending on lower plans |
| **Shopify Email** | Simple campaigns for Shopify stores |

### Permission first: CASL and email lists

For marketing emails (newsletters, promotions), Canada's anti-spam law requires **consent**. The gold standard is **express consent**:

- A clear request ("Get our monthly specials by email"), saying **who** is asking and **why**.
- An **unticked** checkbox or a separate signup; never pre-ticked.
- Records of when and how each person signed up.
- Every email includes sender identification and a working **unsubscribe**.

Some **implied consent** exists (for example, a customer who bought in the last two years), but it expires. Use **double opt-in** (a confirmation email) to keep lists clean and provable.

### Growing the list

- An offer worth signing up for: 10% off, a free guide, a birthday treat, early access.
- Signup points: website popup (after a few seconds or on exit), footer, checkout, in-store QR code, booking forms, social bio link.

### The automated flows

| Flow | Trigger | Emails | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Welcome** | Joins the list | 2–4 over a week | Welcome + offer → story → best sellers or services → reminder |
| **Abandoned cart / browse** (e-commerce) | Leaves without buying | 2–3 | "Still thinking?" → answer objections → small incentive |
| **Post-purchase / post-service** | Buys or job completed | 2–3 | Thank you → how to get the most from it → review request |
| **Win-back** | No purchase in 60–120 days | 2 | "We miss you" → offer |
| **Birthday / anniversary** | Date field | 1 | A treat |
| **Seasonal reminder** (services) | Date since last service | 1–2 | "It's been a year since your furnace tune-up" |

### Campaigns

Send regular campaigns (weekly or monthly). One main message per email, a clear subject line, a preview line that adds to it, short paragraphs, one main button, and mobile-friendly design.

### Segmentation

Send the right email to the right people: new vs repeat customers, location, interests, past purchases, engagement. Segmented emails get more clicks and fewer unsubscribes.

### Deliverability: reaching the inbox

- **Authenticate the sending domain** with **SPF**, **DKIM** and **DMARC** records (your email platform gives you the values). Since 2024, Gmail and Yahoo require these for bulk senders, along with **one-click unsubscribe** and keeping spam complaints very low (under 0.3%). Do it for every client regardless of size.
- Send from the client's domain (not a free Gmail address).
- Clean the list: remove addresses that bounce and people who haven't opened or clicked in 6–12 months (after a re-engagement attempt).

### Metrics

Open rates are unreliable since Apple's Mail Privacy Protection preloads emails. Focus on **clicks**, **conversions**, **revenue per recipient**, **unsubscribe rate** and **spam complaints**.

### SMS

Text messages have huge open rates but need **express consent** under CASL too, and should be used sparingly (order updates, appointment reminders, rare high-value offers).

## D26 | Landing pages and conversion rate optimisation | 3.5 | Landing pages & CRO
@goal Build landing pages that turn ad and social traffic into leads, and improve conversion rates with research and testing.
@lesson
You can double a client's results without spending a cent more on ads, just by converting more of the visitors they already get. That's **conversion rate optimisation (CRO)**.

### Message match

The page must continue the conversation the ad started. If the ad says "$129 furnace tune-up, book this week", the page headline must say the same, with the same offer and visuals. Sending ad traffic to a homepage is one of the most common (and expensive) mistakes in small business marketing.

### Anatomy of a high-converting landing page

1. **Headline** that matches the ad and states the benefit.
2. **Subheadline** with the key details (price, area, speed).
3. **Primary call to action** visible without scrolling (button, phone, short form).
4. **Hero image or video** that shows the real result.
5. **Trust signals:** real review stars and quotes, licences, years in business, guarantees, recognisable logos.
6. **Benefits** (not features) in short sections.
7. **How it works** in three steps.
8. **Objection handling:** FAQs about price, timing, process.
9. **Repeat the CTA** after each major section.
10. **No distractions:** remove the main menu and unrelated links on ad landing pages.

### Forms

Every extra field costs leads. Ask only what's needed to follow up: name, phone or email, and one qualifying question. Add a short line on what happens next ("We'll call within 30 minutes").

### Speed and mobile

Most paid social traffic is mobile. Aim for a page that loads in under 3 seconds on mobile data, with tap-to-call buttons and a form that's easy to fill with one thumb.

### The CRO process

1. **Research:** watch session recordings and heatmaps with **Microsoft Clarity** (free) or Hotjar; read the client's reviews and sales call notes; check GA4 funnel drop-offs.
2. **Hypothesis:** "Because recordings show people scrolling past the form to read reviews, moving reviews above the form will increase leads."
3. **Test:** make the change, ideally as an A/B test.
4. **Learn and repeat.**

### A/B testing, realistically

A/B tests split traffic between two versions. They need enough conversions to be meaningful: as a rule of thumb, aim for at least 100 conversions per version before trusting a result. Small local sites rarely have that, so for them:

- Make **bold** changes (new headline and offer), not tiny ones (button colour).
- Compare periods carefully (same season, same ad spend).
- Use qualitative research (recordings, calls) as your main guide.

Tools for testing: VWO, Optimizely, Convert, or landing page builders like Unbounce that include A/B testing.

### Tools for building pages

The SchoolAura templates, WordPress with a page builder, Webflow, Framer, Shopify (for stores), Unbounce or Leadpages. Choose what the client can maintain.

## D27 | Finding clients | 3.5 | Finding clients
@goal Build a repeatable system for finding clients: warm network, the mini-audit method, compliant cold outreach, LinkedIn, local networking and referrals.
@lesson
Every agency's biggest problem in year one is the same: not enough clients. The fix isn't luck, it's a **daily outreach system** you run whether you feel like it or not.

### The pipeline maths

Work backwards from your goal:

`Clients needed ÷ close rate = sales calls needed`
`Sales calls ÷ reply-to-call rate = replies needed`
`Replies ÷ reply rate = personal outreaches needed`

Example: 2 new clients a month, a 40% close rate → 5 calls → if half of replies book a call, 10 replies → at a 15% reply rate for well-researched personal outreach, about 65–70 outreaches a month, or **3–4 a day**.

### Channel 1: your warm network

Tell 30 people what you do, specifically: *"I help home-service companies in London get more calls from Google."* Ask: "Who do you know who'd want that?" Your first clients very often come from here.

### Channel 2: the mini-audit (Loom) method

The most effective cold outreach for agencies: **give value first**.

1. Pick a high-scoring prospect from your Day 14 list.
2. Spend 15 minutes finding 3 specific problems and 1 quick win (from Search Console-style checks, their Google Business Profile, ads, reviews, site speed).
3. Record a **3-minute Loom** walking through them, showing their own site.
4. Email it with a short, personal message.

### A cold email that respects CASL

> **Subject:** 3 quick fixes for Northfield's Google listing
>
> Hi Dave,
>
> I was looking for an emergency plumber in Byron last night and noticed three things that are probably costing you calls: your Google profile is missing the "Water heater installation" category, 14 of your service pages are hidden from Google, and your best reviews aren't on your website.
>
> I recorded a 3-minute video showing exactly what I found and how to fix it, free to use whether we work together or not: [Loom link]
>
> If it's useful, I'm happy to walk you through it on a 15-minute call.
>
> Riya Shah · Maple & Pixel · 100 Example St, London ON · mapleandpixel.ca
> Not relevant? Reply "no thanks" or [unsubscribe] and I won't email again.

It's relevant to his role, uses his published business email, identifies the sender and includes an unsubscribe (Day 14).

### Follow-up cadence

Most replies come after a follow-up. A polite sequence: **Day 0** email → **Day 3** short follow-up ("did the video come through?") → **Day 7** a different value point → **Day 14** a friendly close ("I'll stop here, the video's yours to keep"). Then stop.

### Channel 3: LinkedIn

- Optimised profile (Day 4) and two posts a week about your niche.
- Connect with owners in your niche with a short, personal note. Engage with their posts before pitching.
- Message only after a real interaction; offer the mini-audit.
- Don't use automation tools that break LinkedIn's terms; accounts get restricted.

### Channel 4: local and partnerships

- **Chamber of commerce**, BIA events, BNI or similar referral groups, trade association meetups.
- **Referral partners** who serve the same clients but don't compete: web designers, accountants, printers, sign makers, business coaches. Offer a referral fee or a reciprocal arrangement.

### Channel 5: content and inbound

Post useful content for your niche (case studies, quick tips, before/after results). It's slower, but it makes outreach warmer: prospects check your profile and see proof.

### Run it daily

Block 60–90 minutes every morning: research 3 prospects, record 3 Looms, send emails, do follow-ups, comment on 5 LinkedIn posts. Track everything in your CRM pipeline.

## D28 | Sales calls, proposals, pricing and contracts | 3.5 | Sales & contracts
@goal Run a discovery call that uncovers what a client really needs, send a proposal that closes, and protect yourself with a clear contract.
@lesson
Selling isn't pushing. A good sales call is a diagnosis: you ask questions, understand the problem and the numbers, and recommend a plan, even if that plan is "you don't need us yet".

### The discovery call (30 minutes)

| Part | Time | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Rapport and agenda | 2 min | "Here's what I'd suggest for the call; anything you want to make sure we cover?" |
| Current situation | 8 min | "Where do new customers come from today? What have you tried? What worked and didn't?" |
| Goals | 5 min | "What would a great year look like? How many more customers a month?" |
| Numbers | 5 min | Average job or order value, close rate, capacity, busy and slow seasons, current marketing spend |
| Obstacles and decision | 5 min | "What's stopped you so far? Who else is involved in the decision? What's your timing?" |
| Next step | 5 min | Summarise their problem in their words, explain how you'd approach it, agree when you'll send a proposal |

Listen more than you talk (aim for 70/30). Write down their exact words; you'll use them in the proposal.

### Handling common objections

| They say | You can say |
|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | "Compared with what? If this brings 10 extra jobs a month at $450, how does that compare with the fee?" Or offer a smaller starting scope. |
| "We tried marketing before; it didn't work." | "What did you try, and how was it measured?" Then explain what you'll do differently and how you'll report. |
| "I need to think about it." | "Of course. What would you need to see to feel confident?" Agree a follow-up date. |
| "My nephew does our social." | "Great. Would it help if we focused on Google and ads, and gave him a content plan?" |

### The proposal

Send within 24–48 hours, while they're still excited. Keep it short (3–6 pages):

1. **Their situation**, in their words
2. **Goals** with numbers
3. **The plan:** what you'll do in the first 90 days
4. **Options:** three tiers (good, better, best), with the middle one recommended
5. **Investment:** monthly fee, setup fee if any, ad spend paid separately
6. **Timeline and what you need from them**
7. **Next step:** sign and pay the first month, kickoff call booked

### The contract

Use a written agreement for every client. Have a lawyer in your province review your template once. It should cover:

- **Scope and deliverables** (exactly what's included, and what isn't)
- **Term:** e.g. an initial 3 months, then month to month with 30 days' notice
- **Fees and payment:** billed monthly in advance; late payment terms; ad spend paid directly by the client
- **Approvals and revisions:** deadlines and rounds included
- **Ownership:** the client owns their accounts, data and ad accounts; content transfers to the client once paid
- **Confidentiality** and **data handling**
- **No guarantees** of specific rankings or results (platforms and algorithms aren't under your control)
- **Limitation of liability**
- **Termination** and handover of access
- **Governing law** (your province)

### Getting paid

Invoice through **Wave**, QuickBooks or Stripe, **monthly in advance**, by card or pre-authorised debit. Once registered for HST, add it to invoices (13% in Ontario). Never start work before the first payment clears.

## D29 | Onboarding, delivery and client reporting | 3.5 | Onboarding & reporting
@goal Onboard new clients smoothly, run delivery like a professional team, report results that build trust, and keep clients for years.
@lesson
Winning a client is the start, not the finish. The agencies that grow are the ones clients never want to leave. That comes from a great first 30 days, reliable delivery and clear communication.

{{fig:dm-lifecycle|The client lifecycle. Retention lives in the right-hand side of this loop.}}

### The onboarding checklist

Send within an hour of the signed contract:

- **Welcome email** with what happens next and the kickoff booking link
- **Onboarding form:** business details, goals, offers, brand assets, competitors, FAQs, who approves what
- **Access requests** (the client owns everything; you get user access):

| Platform | How the client gives access |
|---|---|
| Google Business Profile | Add your email as a Manager |
| Google Analytics 4 | Admin → Property access management → add you |
| Search Console | Settings → Users and permissions → add you |
| Google Ads | Accept a link request from your Manager Account |
| Google Tag Manager | Admin → User management → add you |
| Meta (Facebook, Instagram, ads) | Add your business portfolio as a partner |
| Website (WordPress, Shopify, Wix) | Create a user or collaborator account for you |
| Email platform | Invite you as a user |

### The kickoff call (45–60 minutes)

Confirm goals and the numbers that define success, agree the first 30-day plan, set communication rules (one channel, response times, approval deadlines), and record **baseline numbers** (calls, leads, traffic, reviews, followers) so you can show progress later.

### Delivering like a team, even if you're one person

- **Project management:** a board per client in ClickUp, Trello or Notion with recurring monthly tasks (content, reports, optimisation, reviews).
- **SOPs:** a written or Loom checklist for every repeated task (Day 30).
- **A weekly rhythm:**

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reports sent and reviewed; plan the month |
| 2 | Create and schedule content; optimise campaigns |
| 3 | Approvals for next month; new tests |
| 4 | Content production for next month; account health checks |

### Reporting that builds trust

Monthly: a one-page dashboard plus a 3–5 minute Loom (Days 11, 21 and 24). Quarterly: a **quarterly business review** (QBR) call: results vs goals, what you learned, the plan for next quarter, and new opportunities.

Always tell bad news early and with a plan: "Leads dropped 18% this month because of X; here's what we're doing."

### Keeping clients

- **Early wins** in the first 30 days (indexing fixes, profile optimisation, a quick campaign).
- **Proactive ideas**, not just executing tasks.
- **Remember the business**, not just the account: their busy season, staff changes, new services.
- **Upsell from results:** "Google Ads is bringing 20 leads a month; email could turn past customers into repeat jobs."

### Scope creep

When a client asks for something outside the agreement, say yes to the idea and send a **change request** with the extra cost or a swap ("We can do the event poster this month instead of two carousels").

### Ending well

If a client leaves, hand over access and files promptly and kindly, ask for feedback, and ask if they'd still recommend you. Many come back, and many refer others.

## D30 | Systems, AI and scaling your agency | 3.5 | Systems & scaling
@goal Turn your work into systems, use automation and AI responsibly, build a team, track agency health, and write your 90-day growth plan.
@lesson
You've learned every core service an agency offers. The final skill is building a business that doesn't depend on you doing everything yourself.

### SOPs: write it once, delegate forever

A **standard operating procedure** is a step-by-step checklist for a repeated task. Easiest method: record a Loom while you do the task, then write the steps underneath. Start with:

- New client onboarding · GBP optimisation · Monthly SEO report · Social content month · Google Ads weekly check · Review reply routine · Offboarding

Store them in Notion or Google Docs, one page each, and update them when something changes.

### Automation

Use **Zapier** or **Make** to remove busywork:

| When this happens | Do this automatically |
|---|---|
| A contract is signed | Create the client folder, board from template, and send the welcome email |
| A lead fills a client's form | Add to their CRM, text the owner, send the customer a confirmation |
| A new 5-star review arrives | Post it to the client's Slack and add it to a "testimonials" sheet |
| The 1st of the month | Create report tasks for every client |
| An invoice is overdue | Send a polite reminder |

### AI in agency work

AI speeds up research, outlines, first drafts, ad variations, reporting summaries, scripts and code. Rules that protect your clients and your reputation:

- **A human checks everything** before it reaches a client or goes live.
- **Confidentiality:** don't paste client private data into AI tools unless your agreement and the tool's business terms allow it.
- **Be open** with clients about how you use AI, and charge for outcomes, not keystrokes.
- **Originality:** add real experience, data and voice. Generic AI content is easy to spot and easy to replace.

### Building a team

Hire in this order, usually as **contractors** first: a video editor or designer → a virtual assistant (scheduling, reporting, admin) → specialists (ads, SEO, copywriting) → an account manager. In Canada, whether someone is a contractor or an employee depends on the real working relationship (control, tools, chance of profit or loss, integration), not just what the contract says, so check the CRA's guidance or ask an accountant.

**White-label partners** (other agencies who do work under your brand) can fill gaps, such as web development or link building, while you focus on clients.

### Agency health metrics

| Metric | Meaning | Healthy target |
|---|---|---|
| **MRR** | Monthly recurring revenue from retainers | Growing month on month |
| **Churn** | % of clients lost per month | Under 3–5% |
| **Average revenue per client** | MRR ÷ clients | Rising as you upsell and raise prices |
| **Gross margin** | (Revenue − delivery costs) ÷ revenue | 50%+ |
| **Utilisation** | Billable hours ÷ available hours | 70–80% (leave room for sales and systems) |
| **Client lifetime** | Average months a client stays | 12+ months |

Raise prices for new clients as demand grows, and review existing clients' pricing once a year with notice.

### Your 90-day plan

Three blocks of 30 days. Each with one main goal, three key actions and one number. Keep it on one page and review it every Friday.
